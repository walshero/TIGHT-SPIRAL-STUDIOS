STAGECRAFT CORPUS

The devices Dress Rehearsal's 27 cards are drawn from: what a stage can carry, and the different ways a staging can fail.

The variation rule. Each card names one device below. In any one slot and kind (for example, MOVE / TELL), the three scenes must use three different devices.

The size rule. Every SHOW card must read from Row K. If it needs a close-up (a brass plate, a sticker, a good can opener), it belongs under I7, not SHOW.

The tempt rule. A wrong card has to look like a reasonable choice to a student. The failure is the lesson, so it must be a failure people actually make (Saunders: the trap has to tempt).

1. TELL: the secret gets announced

Each of these puts the secret in words, or in a gesture that stands for words. The house is informed instead of persuaded. Actors call the gesture version indicating: showing the audience a feeling instead of pursuing a goal (Uta Hagen, Respect for Acting; Sanford Meisner, On Acting).

T1 · Placard

A sign, banner or caption hung on the set that names the secret.

Source: Brecht hung scene titles on purpose, to stop the audience getting absorbed. Used by accident, it does the same thing.

In the game: Ernesto's WELCOME BACK banner.

T2 · Costume slogan

The character wears the secret: a T-shirt, a badge, a name tag.

Source: costume design, where clothes signal character. A slogan is the shortcut that skips the signal.

Example: Frank's T-shirt reads WORLD'S BEST CAT DAD.

T3 · The aside

The character turns to the house and says or mouths the secret.

Source: the Elizabethan aside, a real convention (Shakespeare's villains confide in us). It works when the play is built on it; in a realistic scene it breaks the frame.

In the game: Ernesto mouths SMALL. SPACES. NO.

T4 · Charade gesture

A mime that spells out a word or object: rocking an invisible baby, miming handcuffs.

Source: indicating (Hagen, Meisner). The gesture is aimed at the audience, not at anyone in the scene.

In the game: Frank rocks an invisible kitten.

T5 · Exhibit to the house

The character holds up a document, photo or headline so the audience can read it.

Source: the opposite of Hagen's object work. The object is shown instead of used.

Example: Nicky pulls a crumpled flyer from his pocket, MISSING: SCHOOL CELLO, holds it up to the house, then stuffs it back.

T6 · Telephone exposition

A phone rings and the character explains the situation to the caller, and to us.

Source: a stock opening of the drawing-room play. Mamet's memo to the writers of The Unit warns against scenes that exist only to pass information to the audience.

In the game: Nicky's call, "about to sell the hot cello."

T7 · "As you know"

One character tells another something they both already know.

Source: the maid-and-butler opening, where two servants explain the household to each other. Mamet's Unit memo again.

In the game: Ernesto's "As you know, Diane."

T8 · Confession to a confidant

The character says the secret aloud to someone in the scene, then asks them to keep it.

Source: the confidant role in French neoclassical tragedy, a listener who exists so the hero can explain himself.

In the game: Frank's "I love that cat. Tell no one."

T9 · The unprompted denial

The character denies the secret before anyone has raised it.

Source: Gertrude in Hamlet spots the player queen protesting too much. Played straight, the denial is just the secret with a "not" in it.

In the game: Nicky's "I didn't steal it, okay?"

T10 · Mugging

Behavior pushed into cartoon so the audience cannot miss it: tiptoeing like a burglar, wringing hands.

Source: melodrama acting; Meisner's warning against playing the result instead of doing the action.

Example: Nicky creeps around the shop on tiptoe, hood up, glancing over his shoulder.

T11 · The sound underline

A sound or light cue that points at the secret: a siren, a sting, a red light.

Source: melodrama's musical stings. The production says what the actor should have played.

Example: a siren passes; Nicky drops behind the counter.

2. INVISIBLE: true, but no seat can see it

These are real parts of the character that the stage cannot carry. Film can cut to a close-up; a novel can enter a mind; a stage has only bodies, objects, sound and space (Mamet, On Directing Film, on telling the story through what can be seen).

I1 · Backstory memory

Something that happened before the scene. Example: the hallway closet, summer of 1998.

I2 · Labeled feeling

An emotion named instead of played. Example: the warm feeling in Frank's chest.

I3 · Body state

Pulse, adrenaline, a dry mouth: real, and invisible past Row A. Example: Ernesto's pulse climbs to 140.

I4 · Private decision

A choice made in silence that changes nothing on stage yet. Example: Frank decides to buy a collar tomorrow.

I5 · Thought voiceover

A line marked (thinking). The stage has no inner-voice channel unless the play builds one, as O'Neill does on purpose in Strange Interlude.

I6 · Too quiet to hear

A line spoken under the breath, too low for anyone past the front row. Example: NICKY (under his breath): "It was just sitting there."

I7 · Too small to see

A real, visible detail at the wrong scale: an engraved plate, a sticker, a brand of can opener. The founder's Row K test catches these. Example: initials carved inside the cello's f-hole.

I8 · Typed and deleted

The character writes the secret into a phone and deletes it unsent. The house sees thumbs, not words. Example: Frank types "who's a good girl" to no one, and deletes it.

I9 · Against the circumstances

A clear, stageable action that argues the opposite of the secret. Every seat sees it, and it tells them the wrong story.

Source: Stanislavski's given circumstances. Every action has to be true to the facts of the scene.

In the game: Nicky rosins the bow like a pro. Only an owner can do that. (This card sits in the INVISIBLE slot, since the game has three kinds.)

3. SHOW: the house works it out

Each of these gives the audience evidence and lets them put it together. Hitchcock's bomb under the table is the model: once the audience knows what a character does not, every ordinary moment fills with suspense (Truffaut, Hitchcock). Here, the house learns the secret and the other characters never do.

Objects

S1 · The endowed prop

An object the character brought, that only makes sense if the secret is true.

Source: Hagen's endowment, an object charged with history.

In the game: Ernesto's door wedge.

S2 · Handling

How a character carries or treats an object shows their relation to it. Owners flinch; thieves do not.

Source: Hagen's object exercises.

In the game: Nicky drags the cello; Sol stands it up carefully.

S3 · Set as evidence

The room itself has been arranged by the secret.

Source: Ibsen's and Chekhov's rooms, where the furniture holds the history.

In the game: Frank's blanket bed and tuna pyramid.

Moves

S4 · Avoidance in space

The body moves away from the thing it fears, step by step.

Source: Viewpoints, spatial relationship (Bogart and Landau, The Viewpoints Book).

In the game: Ernesto drifts to the stairwell door.

S5 · The task they cannot do

A simple task the character should be able to do, failed in front of us.

Source: Stanislavski's method of physical actions. Truth lives in doing a task, so failing the task shows the lie.

In the game: Nicky cannot hold the cello.

S6 · Caught and cover

Interrupted mid-action, the character covers with something absurd.

Source: farce, from Feydeau on.

In the game: Frank drinks the cat's milk.

S7 · The status move

Who looks away, who waits, who fills the silence.

Source: Keith Johnstone, Impro, on status.

Example: Sol lets the silence run; Nicky cannot stand it.

Lines

S8 · Bravado

The line says the opposite of what is true, loudly.

Source: subtext (Stanislavski); Mamet, Three Uses of the Knife, on characters who never say what they want.

In the game: Ernesto's "Eleven floors is nothing."

S9 · The tactic that gives it away

The character pursues what they want, and how they pursue it betrays them.

Source: Mamet's objective. Every character wants something, and the way they go after it is the drama.

In the game: Nicky bids against himself.

S10 · The slip

A detail the character should not know leaks into an ordinary line.

Source: comic slips of the tongue, from Sheridan's Mrs. Malaprop on.

In the game: Frank's "especially at 9am."

S11 · The pause

What is not said, held long enough to be heard.

Source: Pinter's pauses and silences.

Example: Sol's silence between Nicky's prices.
