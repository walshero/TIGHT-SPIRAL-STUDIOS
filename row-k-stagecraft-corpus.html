STAGECRAFT CORPUS

Version 2. The devices Dress Rehearsal's cards are drawn from, each traced to a source or marked as a studio invention.

Variation. In any one slot and kind (for example, MOVE / TELL), the three scenes use three different devices.

Row K. A SHOW card must read from the back row. Anything that needs a close-up is I7, not SHOW.

The silent-movie test (Mamet, R2). An OBJECT or MOVE card is SHOW only if it works with the sound off.

Tempt. A wrong card must be a mistake students actually make. The failure is the lesson.

1. The spine: Aristotle's ladder

The oldest account of this game is Aristotle's ranking of how a play lets someone discover the truth (Poetics ch. 16, R5). Worst to best:

  1. Signs and tokens. A scar, a necklace, an object that proves who someone is. Least artful, especially when produced as proof.
  2. Contrived by the poet. The character simply announces the truth, as Orestes does.
  3. Memory. An object or sound sets off visible feeling, as when Odysseus weeps at the lyre.
  4. Reasoning. Someone draws the conclusion from evidence.
  5. From the incidents. The truth comes out of the action itself, as in Oedipus. Best.

The studio maps the game onto the ladder (our mapping, not Aristotle's): TELL cards live on rungs 1 and 2, and SHOW cards on rungs 4 and 5: the house reasons from what the action gives it. Hitchcock adds why it works: once the audience knows something a character does not, every ordinary moment fills with suspense (R6). In this game the house learns the secret and the other people on stage never do.

2. TELL: the secret is announced

Spolin's definition, which this game teaches: telling is indicating, in words or any other indirect way, instead of doing (R1).

T1 · Placard

A sign, banner or caption that names the secret. Brecht used placards on purpose, to break the illusion (R14); here it breaks it by accident.

Source: R14. In the game: Ernesto's banner.

T2 · Costume slogan

The character wears the secret on a shirt or badge.

STUDIO INVENTION. No source found.

T3 · The aside

The character speaks or mouths the secret to the house, unheard by the others onstage. A real convention (R13); in a realistic scene it breaks the frame.

Source: R13. In the game: Ernesto mouths SMALL. SPACES. NO.

T4 · Expressive gesture

A mime performed for the house instead of behavior done in the scene. Spolin calls this telling: indicating instead of doing (R1). The nearest Viewpoints term is expressive gesture, symbolic movement for an inner state, as opposed to ordinary behavioral gesture (R11).

Source: R1, R11. In the game: Frank rocks an invisible kitten.

T5 · Token as proof

A document or object produced to prove the secret, held up or hung where the house can read it. Aristotle's least artful recognition (R5, rung 1).

Source: R5.

T6 · Telling the listener

A character explains the situation to someone so the audience hears it. Mamet names this as a warning sign (R2).

Source: R2. In the game: Nicky's phone call. (No source was found for phone exposition as a named convention.)

T7 · "As you know"

Two characters tell each other what they both know. Sometimes called maid-and-butler dialogue; Sheridan mocked it in The Critic and Stoppard in The Real Inspector Hound (R12).

Source: R12, R2. In the game: Ernesto's "As you know, Diane."

T8 · The announcement

The character states the secret outright to someone onstage. Aristotle's second rung: Orestes says who he is (R5).

Source: R5. In the game: Frank's "I love that cat."

T9 · The unprompted denial

The character denies the secret before anyone raises it, which in modern usage reads as guilt.

STUDIO INVENTION. The modern idiom only. Shakespeare's Gertrude meant something else: in his time "protest" meant vow (R19). In the game: Nicky's "I didn't steal it."

T10 · Mugging

Behavior blown up past what the moment would cause, so no one can miss it: cartoon tiptoes, wrung hands. Meisner calls a response out of proportion to its cause false (R7).

Source: R7. The name "mugging" is common studio usage, not from the source.

T11 · The sound underline

A sound or light cue that points at the secret: a siren, a sting.

STUDIO INVENTION. No source found.

3. INVISIBLE: true, but no seat can get it

Hagen: emotion follows from pursuing an objective (R8). Stanislavski: every objective carries the seed of an action (R9). Mamet: the story travels through what can be seen (R3). A feeling has to come out as an action to reach the house.

I1 · Untriggered memory

Something from before the scene, with nothing onstage to set it off and nothing visible as a result. It fails because only what can be seen carries (R3). Aristotle shows the fix: a trigger and a visible response (R5, rung 3; see S10).

Source: R3; fix from R5. In the game: Ernesto's closet; Nicky's band room.

I2 · Named feeling

An emotion labeled instead of played (R8).

Source: R8. In the game: the warm feeling in Frank's chest.

I3 · Body state

Pulse, adrenaline, a dry mouth. Real, and invisible past the front row.

Source: R3, on carrying the story through what can be seen. In the game: Ernesto's pulse.

I4 · Private decision

A choice that produces no action yet. Stanislavski: every objective has to carry the seed of an action (R9).

Source: R9. In the game: Frank decides to buy a collar.

I5 · Spoken thought

A line marked (thinking). O'Neill built a whole play on it in Strange Interlude, on purpose (R15). This game is realism, so the house cannot hear it.

Source: R15. In the game: all three scenes' LINE / INVISIBLE cards.

I6 · Too quiet to carry

A line spoken under the breath, below what reaches the house.

STUDIO INVENTION. Lead to research: stage voice and projection training.

I7 · Token too small

A real, visible proof at a scale no seat can read: an engraved plate, a sticker, a carved initial. Aristotle's tokens (R5, rung 1), failed again by the founder's Row K test.

Source: R5 and the Row K test.

I8 · Typed and deleted

The character types the secret into a phone and deletes it. The house sees thumbs.

STUDIO INVENTION.

I9 · Against the given circumstances

A clear action that contradicts the facts of the scene. Every seat sees it, and it argues the wrong story (R9).

Source: R9. In the game: Nicky rosins the bow like a pro. It sits in the INVISIBLE slot because the game has three kinds.

4. SHOW: the house works it out

Objects

S1 · The planted prop

An object onstage that only makes sense if the secret is true. Chekhov: don't bring a rifle on unless someone means to fire it (R16). The house reasons from it, Aristotle's fourth rung (R5).

Source: R16, R5. In the game: Ernesto's door wedge. (v1 called this Hagen's endowment; that was wrong.)

S2 · Handling

How a character treats an object tells us what it is to them.

Source: related to Hagen's endowment, where the actor's behavior gives an object qualities like heat or weight (R8). Applying it to ownership and worth is STUDIO FRAMING. In the game: Nicky drags the cello; Sol stands it up with care.

S3 · Where as evidence

The place has been arranged by the secret. Spolin's Where: place and relationship come through the body and the environment (R1).

Source: R1. In the game: Frank's blanket bed and tuna pyramid.

Moves

S4 · Spatial relationship

Distance between bodies and objects, changing over time. A body moving away from what it fears (R11).

Source: R11. In the game: Ernesto drifts to the stairwell door.

S5 · The task they cannot do

An ordinary task, actually attempted and failed in front of us. Meisner's reality of doing (R7); Stanislavski's physical action (R9).

Source: R7, R9. In the game: Nicky cannot hold the cello.

S6 · Caught and cover

A near-discovery and a desperate deflection. Feydeau's farce runs on it (R18).

Source: R18. In the game: Frank drinks the cat's milk.

S7 · Status

Who holds eye contact, who goes still, who takes space (R10).

Source: R10.

S8 · The reaction

The information travels through the other character's response. Mamet builds scenes by setting plain images side by side (R3); Meisner: truth lives in the response (R7).

Source: R3, R7. In the game: the cello hits the frame; Sol looks, Nicky doesn't.

S9 · Repetition and tempo

The same small action again, or at the wrong speed (R11).

Source: R11.

S10 · The trigger

An object or sound onstage sets off a visible response. Aristotle's third rung, done right (R5).

Source: R5. Example: the shop radio plays a cello piece; Nicky reaches over and switches it off.

Lines

S11 · Speech as a smokescreen

The line says the opposite of the truth, loudly. Pinter's second kind of silence: a flood of talk covering what is not said (R17).

Source: R17. In the game: Ernesto's "Eleven floors is nothing."

S12 · The tactic that betrays them

The character pursues what they want, and how they pursue it gives them away. Mamet: drama is the pursuit of a goal against an obstacle (R2).

Source: R2. In the game: Nicky bids against himself.

S13 · The slip

A slip in an ordinary line reveals what the character wants. Michael Fontaine reads the slips in Plautus's Menaechmi this way (R20).

Source: R20. In the game: Frank's "especially at 9am." (v1 credited Mrs. Malaprop; a malapropism is a wrong word, not a revealing slip.)

S14 · The pause

What is not said, held long enough to be heard. Pinter also warned against pauses played for portent (R17).

Source: R17. Example: Sol's silence between Nicky's prices.
