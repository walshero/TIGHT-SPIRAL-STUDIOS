EN195 Creative Writing, fiction workshop only. Written 2026-09-24 from two founder

**The sentence.** The player sits in a fiction workshop as an Asker, notices what a peer
draft is actually doing, and then says it in a way that helps the writer. The player
learns that a true notice said badly still fails the writer.

Six places in this spec wait on you. Each one is marked **YOUR CALL** and carries a
recommendation, so you can answer "go" or change it.

## 1. The frame

The game borrows its roles from Jesse Ball, "The Asking," in *Notes on My Dunce Cap*
(your EN195 course materials).

- **The Asker** is the player.
- **The Asked** is the writer. She sits at a round table with the class, holding her pages.
- **The Advocate** sits beside her and rules on every card the instant it lands. The
  Advocate is an unnamed role, as in Ball, because students will play the role
  themselves in real workshop. Her timing copies Fran in Dress Rehearsal: she answers
  the card in your hand before anything else happens. Her voice stays procedural, not
  folksy. She says "That isn't a question. Please reframe," not "Oh, hon."
- **The Recorder** writes down every question and none of the answers (Ball, rule 12).
  Her page is the only record the game keeps.

The text on the table is always a peer draft, and every comment speaks of the narrator,
never the writer (Pat Schneider, Amherst Writers & Artists).

## 2. Two layers, scored apart

Students fail at noticing and at saying separately, so the game tests them separately.

1. **Notice.** Play a card that names what is on the page.
2. **Say it.** Play a card with the words you would say to the Asked.

A right notice followed by a wrong feedback card still misses. The Advocate says so,
and that moment carries more teaching than any other in the game.

## 3. One call, step by step

1. The Advocate calls on you.
2. You play a noticing card. The Advocate rules on it.
3. If the notice holds, the evidence line in the draft gets a pencil underline, and you
   get a hand of feedback cards.
4. You play a feedback card. If it helps, the Asked answers out loud, the Recorder
   writes your words down, and the Advocate holds the silence.
5. The Advocate calls on you again. A draft gets three calls. It ends early once you
   have asked about every load-bearing notice.

**The struck card keeps its note (founder ruling, 2026-09-25).** When you play a
noticing card, its test line turns into your claim: "Claim:" appears in front of it.
If the Advocate strikes the card, the name gets a line through it, the word Struck
appears, and the Advocate's reason stays printed on the card under "The Advocate:"
for the rest of the draft. A struck card stays struck in later calls. An upheld card
shows "Upheld. Asked already." with the Advocate's reason. Struck feedback cards keep
their note the same way.

**The reframe rule** comes straight from Ball, rule 7. A missed card gets struck, and
you reframe once. Miss again, and the Advocate says "Let's hear from someone else," a
hand goes up in the dark, and the call ends with nothing on the Recorder's page.

**The silence** comes from Ball, rule 8. After a good question and its answer, the
room holds still for about three and a half seconds. The sunlight softens, dust turns in the
beams, and no card can be played. The game teaches that silence belongs to the writer.

## 4. The noticing deck

The noticing cards come from your handout "Mapping the Storyscape with Writerly Moves."
Every card carries your own test line from that handout, word for word.

- **Writerly Moves (11), what the draft does well:** Anchor in Reality, Character Want,
  Friction / Obstacle, Micro-Tension, World Detail That Matters, Backstory Snap,
  Interior Weather, Relationship Beat, Setting the Stakes, Turn / Shift, Notable Pattern.
- **Observation Tags (10), what the draft may need:** Overpacked Suitcase, Shaky World
  Rules, Floating Camera, Blurry Stakes, Dropped Thread, Emotional Static, Stock Footage
  Moment, Driving Too Fast, Noticeable Info Dump, Convenient Coincidence.

Tags wear a dashed border and a printed label, so the two decks never rely on color.

Each draft deals six noticing cards of four kinds. Only the first kind scores:

| Kind | What it is | What the Advocate does |
|---|---|---|
| Load-bearing | Present, and doing the draft's real work | Rules it true and underlines the evidence |
| Present but minor | On the page, but not the job this draft is doing | Agrees it is there and asks you to look again |
| Plausible bait | Sounds right, and the draft earns its way out of it | Shows you why the draft already earned it |
| False notice | Simply absent, like calling clichés on a draft with none | Tells you what is actually there |

## 5. The feedback deck

Three ways to speak help the writer. They come from your sources:

- **The Schneider register** says what is strong or what stays with you, about the
  narrator and never the writer.
- **The Ball question** is a real question whose answer the Asker cannot know or guess.
- **The contrast note** is your own example: "This felt general and hard to picture,
  but I could really picture the scene with the tricycle."

Five ways to speak fail the writer. The Advocate strikes each one by name:

| Wrong card | Example from the workup | The Advocate says |
|---|---|---|
| Praise with no evidence | "I really loved the opening. It was so good." | That isn't a question, and it doesn't show her what you saw. |
| Censure | "The ending doesn't work." | That is a verdict. |
| The fix-it | "Cut the happy line and have him walk out." | That writes her story for her. Tell her what you noticed. |
| Speculation about the writer | "Did this hike happen to you?" | We speak about the narrator here, never the writer. |
| The disguised opinion | "Don't you think the brother is too nice?" | That is an opinion wearing a question mark. |

The fix-it card comes from your own workshop instruction: readers do not edit.

> **YOUR CALL, D4: statements in a room built for questions.** Ball bans opinions and
> allows only questions (rule 14). Schneider's register and your contrast note are
> statements. The workup lets all three through.
> **Recommended:** keep all three. The game teaches the difference between evidence
> and verdict, and a statement with evidence helps the writer.
> **Tradeoff:** purists of the Asking will notice the blend.
> **Simpler:** questions only, pure Ball.
> **Further:** open with a Schneider round (what is strong) and follow it with an
> Asking round, the way AWA opens with strengths first.

> **YOUR CALL, D5: how the Advocate handles a miss.** The workup follows Ball: reframe
> once, then the Advocate moves on and nothing goes on the Recorder's page.
> **Recommended:** keep Ball's rule. It costs something without a score, and it matches
> what students will do in the room.
> **Tradeoff:** a stuck student loses the call and may not see a good card modeled.
> **Simpler:** unlimited reframes.
> **Further:** after the Advocate moves on, show the card a strong Asker played, as The
> Tell does with its prior reader.

## 7. No score, only the Recorder's page

The game keeps no points, no grade, and no stars (the Osterweil seat). The Recorder's
page lists every card that reached the Asked, and the Asked takes that page home. A
blank page tells the player enough.

## 8. Art and animation

The game follows the EN195 arcade: cut paper, CC0, Tight Spiral Paper Craft, and your
standing Lumino City bar, a handmade miniature set with hand-cut edges and light as a
material. The scene holds more than half of the first screen.

**The room (v0.2, founder note: the hanging lamp read as interrogation).** A sunny
workshop room. Five people sit around a round wooden table with mugs and loose pages:
a classmate, the Advocate, the Asked holding her draft, the Recorder with her notebook,
and another classmate. Two more sit at the near side with their backs to us, so the
player reads as one more seat at the table. Daylight falls from two windows with
plants on the sills, and paper bunting hangs over a board of pinned drafts. In dark
mode the same room sits at sunset.

**Seven moves, all built in the workup:**

1. **Entry.** The paper layers drop into place, back to front.
2. **The card lands.** The card you tap flies up into the room and lands in the middle
   of the table.
3. **The ruling.** The Advocate raises her palm, and her line unrolls on a paper slip.
   A struck card gets a rust X on the table and a line through its words.
4. **The evidence.** A pencil line draws itself under the evidence in the draft.
5. **The answer.** The Asked tilts her head while she thinks, and her answer comes one
   line at a time.
6. **The silence.** The sunlight softens, dust drifts in the beams, and a quiet bar fills.
   Then the Recorder's pen moves and a line of ink draws across her notebook.
7. **Someone else.** A classmate raises a hand when the Advocate moves on. At the end,
   the Asked bows and the class claps.

With Reduce Motion on, every move becomes a cut, and the silence keeps its full length
because the silence is the lesson.

**Layout.** On a phone the room sits on top, then the draft, then the Advocate's slip
right above your cards, so her ruling appears where your thumb is. On a wide screen the
room stays pinned on the left while you read and play on the right.
