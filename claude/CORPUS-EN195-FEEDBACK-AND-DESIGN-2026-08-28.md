## 1. THE OUTCOMES, VERBATIM

Any game claiming EN195 alignment answers to these five. From the syllabus:

> Upon successful completion of EN195, students will be able to:
> 1. **Analyze** the structure, conventions and craft of literary texts in a variety of creative genres;
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-BEGIN Summer 2026 EN195 Syllabus, course objective 2, verbatim -->
> 2. **Compose** original work using appropriate conventions within different genres of creative writing — creative nonfiction, poetry, the 10-minute play, and short fiction;
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-END -->
> 3. **Apply** a variety of processes and strategies for developing and revising original creative work;
> 4. **Provide** relevant, constructive critiques of peers' work using genre-specific vocabulary and principles of craft;
> 5. **Produce** a polished portfolio, including an artist statement that reflects on key influences, aesthetic values, goals and accomplishments.

**Four genres, named: creative nonfiction, poetry, the 10-minute play, short fiction.**

Note what outcome 4 is. A quarter of the stated course is **learning to respond to
someone else's draft in craft vocabulary**. No game in the arcade currently trains it.
That is the largest uncovered outcome in the course.

### The A-criteria, which are the real rubric

DEADLINE · GUIDELINES · **THEME** · **CONVENTIONS** · **CREATIVITY and ORIGINALITY** ·
**DEVELOPMENT** · **CLARITY** · **LANGUAGE** · **CRAFT** · **REVISION**

Two of these are load-bearing for game design and are quoted exactly:

> **LANGUAGE:** work uses concrete images and a variety of sentence structure;
> demonstrates thoughtful word choice and **avoids word packages (clichés)**

> **REVISION:** work shows evidence of significant revision (**reshapes or extends or
> complicates or substantially clarifies** the work)

"Word packages" is his own term for clichés, in his own rubric. And revision is defined
as four specific operations, not as "improve it."

## 2. THE SPINE

Three ideas run through every genre in his feedback. A game that misses these is not
teaching his course.

**1. Images, energy, tension.** From the poetry instructions, citing the course text
(Heather Sellers, *The Practice of Creative Writing*, 4th ed.): *"strong creative
writing often depends on images, energy, and tension."* This is the course's own
three-part test, and it is genre-neutral.

**2. Praxis: notice → experiment → reflect.** His word, used repeatedly. He names it
as the thing that makes growth transferable, and the artist statement is where it gets
assessed.

**3. The writer is the decider.** Stated in the poetry instructions,
*"Writers will always make the final call, and you can help make that call more
informed"*, and repeated in his portfolio notes as **"You, as writer, are always the
decider."**

**This third one is a hard design constraint.** A game that scores a craft judgement
right or wrong contradicts the course's own stance. Feedback describes an effect on a
reader; it does not issue a verdict. The Cliché Hunter "coined" verdict is the only
place in the arcade that currently gets this right.

## 3. THE CORPUS: HIS FEEDBACK MOVES

Each move: what he calls it, his language, what it teaches, and how it could be played.

### 3.1 CROSS-GENRE (these recur in all four)

**THE CAMERA.** His most-used instrument by a wide margin. Feedback is framed as
distance and what is in frame: *move the camera closer*, *the camera is moving too
quickly when I need a slow ride*, *scenes require zooming in further*, *it's okay to
stay zoomed out when you move fast*. And the cut rule: the camera includes things that
matter less, and **if you cut the extra, the important matters more.**
→ *Playable as:* a distance control on a real passage. The move is not "add detail," it
is choosing focal length per beat.

**GENERAL → SPECIFIC.** The single most frequent note in the corpus.
> *"That's like eating 'fruit.' Show me the fairy wings or the death star plans."*

> *"You can lead a horse to water, but you can't tell it to be thirsty."* (on show, don't tell)

He does not say "add description." He says a general word is a **placeholder standing
where a chosen one should be**, and names the replacement class.
→ *Playable as:* substitution, exactly the Cliché Hunter mechanic pointed at abstraction
instead of cliché.

**THE UNIVERSAL AND THE DISTINCT.** A whole diagnosis in one sentence:
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-BEGIN Matt's portfolio feedback, verbatim -->
> *"it's possible that this poem could be true for millions of people... You nail the
> universal — now invite the distinct."*
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-END -->

Not a flaw to fix. A **stage** to move through. The universal is the achievement; the
distinct is the next move.
→ *Playable as:* the strongest thing in this corpus. A line that could belong to anyone
vs. a line only this writer could write.

**WORD PACKETS.** His term, twice, in two registers:
> *"these are word packets we've seen before, so innovate"*
> *"when we drop clichés and allusions to other work, I find writers' voices become
> clearer for the lack of noise, and the risk-taking."*

The claim is not that clichés are ugly. It is that they are **noise that obscures a
voice.** Cliché Hunter currently teaches the first half. The second half, *your voice
gets clearer when the packets go*, is not in it yet.

**ON THE NOSE.** Recurs across play and fiction. The fix is always structural, not
verbal: let the audience arrive at it, do not have a character state it.
→ *Playable as:* pairs, where the same beat is delivered stated vs. inferred.

**AMBIGUITY vs. CONFUSION.** A distinction he draws explicitly and I have not seen
elsewhere:
> *"I advise towards ambiguity about how what happened makes me feel, not about what
> actually just happened, as that's more like confusion."*

→ *Playable as:* sort ambiguities into the two bins. One is craft, one is a defect, and
they look identical on the page.

**SENTENCE RHYTHM AS MIXING.**
> *"Short lines are powerful... but sometimes you do just have to get in there with a
> longer sentence that drills down... Those long ones make the short ones stick even
> harder. When you mix it up, it's like a DJ, so keep mixing."*

### 3.2 POETRY, and a correction worth reading

**The assignments are the "Where I'm From" poem and a free verse poem.** Not metrical
verse. Not fixed forms.

**This matters for the parked instruction.** *"Let's ONLY use enjambment on poems to
show rhyme scheme and meter"* would narrow the poetry lane to something the course does
not assign and does not assess. His own poetry feedback contains **no notes on meter or
rhyme scheme at all**. It is about camera, specificity, titles, line breaks, and the
universal/distinct move. Recommend not resuming the parked instruction as written.

What he actually says about poems:

**TITLES ARE A FREE TOOL.**
> *"Titles can be the keys that make a poem accessible for a reader. Don't pass up free,
> useful, expected, craft-based tools like titles."*

**THE LINE BREAK ALREADY PAUSES.**
> *"I'm not sure you need commas at the end of lines, since the line breaks themselves
> create a natural pause. If you constrain, when you do need one it will matter."*

This is the real enjambment lesson in his course, and it is about **punctuation economy**,
not scansion.

**ENJAMBMENT IS AN AFFORDANCE YOU DO NOT SURRENDER.** On a prose poem:
> *"why give up the tool of enjambment? It's an affordance of the genre, imho it's a
> crime not to use the core tools of craft."*

**THE LINE BREAK AS A UNIT OF MEANING.** On where a line ends, he will say which words
belong together and why the first line must not be readable independently of the second
unless that is the intent.

**ONE WORD CAN CHANGE WHAT A POEM CLAIMS.** The clearest single note in the corpus:
> *"consider dropping 'from.' Then your memory isn't a container or a place; it's an
> active part of your identity."*

→ *This is a whole game.* Delete one word; the poem's claim about the world changes.
Osterweil's content-mechanic identity, exactly: the lesson **is** the mechanic.

### 3.3 THE 10-MINUTE PLAY

He teaches the play **first** in the sequence, deliberately:
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-BEGIN Matt's portfolio feedback, verbatim -->
> *"It's one of the reasons I have students write the play first — the dialogue,
> 'interior weather,' and movement of the characters has been so much better since I
> made that switch."*
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-END -->

**AFTER THE STORM.** His named move: the scene opens *after* the rupture, and we live in
the aftermath. His framing: it is not that a character is generally unsupportive, it is
that a line has just been crossed, and you can feel it has been crossed before.

**DRAMATIC IRONY IS THE AUDIENCE'S, NOT THE CHARACTER'S.**
> *"for dramatic irony, you'd have US have that funny feeling as the audience instead of
> the characters state it directly."*

**DO NOT LET THE PRESSURE COOKER RESET.** On scene breaks that vent tension the script
just built. Paired with: *"when things get crazy, is when you test your characters to see
what they're made of"*, and a plea not to drop the curtain at the moment of maximum
pressure.

**STAGE DIRECTIONS SHOULD NOT NARRATE WHAT DIALOGUE ALREADY DID.** Cut the direction
describing an action the following speech performs.

**CASTING RULE.** A character who never appears and has no line, onstage or off, is not
a character. They are a reference in the script.

> **Note on BLOCKING.** I killed `blocking.html` on your instruction ("too light on
> specs"). This corpus is why it was thin: it was built without these moves in hand.
> The 10-minute play is **one of the four required genres** and now has no game.

### 3.4 CREATIVE NONFICTION

**WORLD DETAILS THAT MATTER.** His named move, and he insists on the qualifier:
> *"So yes, I've settled on World Details that Matter as the play, not just World
> Details."*

The correction is the teaching. Sensory detail without selection is sensory overload,
and he tells a story against himself about over-writing an image in his own workshop
days.

**THE 5% CUT.** His own estimate: the camera includes perhaps 5% that matters less, and
cutting it makes the rest matter more. *"That's taken me about 40 years to get."*

**BACKSTORY SNAP.** A named move: the minimum context delivered fast, rather than an
info dump or deliberate withholding that reads as vagueness.

**SLIDE.** Named move: skip the transactional beats and land on the beat that matters.
Its diagnostic in `writerly-moves` (as *Sliding*): *"My draft explains every movement
between scenes and the energy keeps leaking out."*

**PARAGRAPHS ARE GUIDEPOSTS.**
> *"Paragraphs move the reader along and are critical for the brain as guideposts. Help
> your readers! Think of them as friends."*

### 3.5 SHORT FICTION

**FIRST DOMINO.** Named move: the change is already underway in the first sentence.

**GIVE THE REVEAL ROOM TO LAND.** His most consistent fiction note. A reveal that
arrives too late has no time to do its work: *"why deprive your audience?"* Related:
save the biggest image for where the power is needed, do not spend it early.

**TROPES ARE NOT THE PROBLEM; UNDIFFERENTIATED TROPES ARE.** He praises work that is
fable-like or genre-shaped while noting where it needs *distinct moves*. Same shape as
the universal/distinct move in poetry.

**"I DIDN'T FIND ANYTHING EXTRA."** His highest structural compliment. Worth naming as
the win condition it is.

## 4. THE FOUR FREEDOMS (OSTERWEIL), MAPPED

His signature framework, already the studio's spine (OS §1). Checked against the course
and the arcade honestly.

| freedom | where the COURSE already has it | where the ARCADE fails it |
|---|---|---|
| **Freedom to fail** | Strong. Contract grading adapted from Peter Elbow, in the syllabus: *"meant to allow you to take risks and concentrate on your writing instead of your grade."* | Mostly fine. The cliché cowpath games have timers and fail states, part of why they went to Lab. |
| **Freedom to experiment** | Strong. Four genres, most new to most students; assignments offer choice; he repeatedly credits choice for the best work. | **Weak.** Most arcade games have one correct answer. Experimentation means the system responds to an approach it did not anticipate. |
| **Freedom to fashion identities** | Strong. The artist statement is identity work; "Where I'm From" is explicitly identity. | **Absent.** No game lets a player take a writer's stance and see the world through it. |
| **Freedom of effort** | Strong. Six-week online, self-paced within deadlines. | Mixed. Fixed by removing clocks; Cliché Hunter and Enjambment's still mode do this. |

**Where the arcade is weakest: experiment and identity.** Both are the freedoms hardest
to fake, and both are exactly what a workshop is. Outcome 4 (respond to a peer's draft
in craft vocabulary) sits at the intersection and is uncovered.

## 5. BARCELONA SUMMERS, reviewed as instructed

**What it is.** A student game, built by a student in EN195 Creative Writing (summer
6-week online), credited *Hamish K.* per `student-attribution-standard.md`. Consent on
file; recorded in `approvals-log.md` 2026-08-09. Published on the student's own Netlify
deploy; guest cabinet 6 on `arcade.html`. **The studio does not host it.**

**Why it exists is the interesting part.** It was Matt's *portfolio response*. Instead of
writing the usual end comment, he built a game out of the student's own writerly moves
and asked whether it could be used with future students.

**The design, from the arcade card:**
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-BEGIN arcade.html guest cabinet 6 blurb, verbatim -->
> *"A summer in Barcelona, read three ways. Make the editor's cut — keep what shows, lose
> what tells — and watch a scene carry its meaning without stopping to explain itself."*
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-END -->
> Chip: **"Notice. What the image does that the sentence can't say."**

**What it gets right, and what to take from it:**

1. **The mechanic is a cut, not a quiz.** "Keep what shows, lose what tells" is the
   5% Cut and Show-Don't-Tell made operable. Same family as Cliché Hunter's pull-and-set.
2. **Read three ways** is the poetry instructions' three Ways of Reading, in a game.
   That structure is already course-canonical and is reusable.
3. **The consequence is the text re-forming**, not a score. The scene carries its meaning
   after the cut, so you read the result of your own judgement.
4. **It is built from one writer's moves.** The corpus was a real portfolio. That is why
   it has specificity a generic exercise cannot buy.

**Open, and not a consent problem:** the student's own deploy still shows a full last
name and a date, which the studio standard would HALT on its own surfaces. The arcade
card is compliant; the page it points at is not, and the studio has no write access.

## 6. WRITERLY MOVES: the asset not to reinvent

**Parked by founder ruling 2026-07-11. Built by Matt by hand, without AI.** Its card tags
are the studio thesis: **notice · name · test · use.**

Its **Diagnose** mode is, on the evidence in this repo, the most pedagogically advanced
thing the studio owns. It does not quiz definitions. It takes a revision problem the
writer actually has and routes to the move that fixes it. That is transfer, not recall.

**The open question blocking it is still open: two taxonomies that barely overlap.**

- **Craft Arcade (13):** Sliding · Object Magnet · Pressure Cooker · Misbelief Engine ·
  Story Mirror · Expectation Switch · Negative Space · Significant Detail ·
  Kishōtenketsu Turn · World Rule · Subtext Ladder · Cut to Heat · Attention Ritual
- **Training Quiz (11):** Anchor in Reality · Character Want · Friction/Obstacle ·
  Micro-Tension · World Detail That Matters · Backstory Snap · Interior Weather ·
  Relationship Beat · Setting the Stakes · Turn/Shift · Notable Pattern

**This corpus adds evidence toward an answer.** The moves he actually uses in live
feedback (Camera, Slide, Backstory Snap, After the Storm, First Domino, World Details
That Matter, Pressure Cooker) are **overwhelmingly from the Training Quiz side**, plus
several not in either list. His working vocabulary is scene-beat vocabulary. That is a
data point for the review session, not a decision; the call is his.

## 7. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE NEXT POETRY GAME

Not a design. The inputs a design should answer to.

1. **Build on the universal/distinct move.** It is the highest-value, most repeated,
   most generous note in his corpus, it is genre-portable, and no arcade game touches it.
   It has a built-in freedom to fail: the universal is already an achievement.
2. **The mechanic should be a change to the text that changes its claim.** Drop one word
   and the poem stops being about a place and starts being about an identity. That is
   content-mechanic identity, which is the deepest Osterweil principle.
3. **Do not score craft judgements right or wrong.** Report the effect on a reader.
   "You, as writer, are always the decider" is his rule, not a nicety.
4. **The three Ways of Reading are the natural mode structure** (reader experience,
   craft, questions) because they are already how his students are told to read.
5. **Free verse and "Where I'm From," not meter and rhyme.** Match what the course
   assigns.
6. **Cover the uncovered outcome if you can.** Outcome 4, responding to a draft in craft
   vocabulary, is a quarter of the course with no game. His HOCs/LOCs handout already
   supplies the hierarchy, and his own POOR/BETTER comment pairs are ready-made items.

## 9. HONEST LIMITS OF THIS CORPUS

- **One semester.** Summer 2026, two sections. Earlier feedback exists in Drive
  (`EN195 S16 Portfolio feedback.docx`, `Fall 2025 Portfolio Feedback`) and was not read.
  Patterns here are real but not longitudinal.
- **Portfolio-stage feedback only.** These are end comments on finished portfolios. His
  in-line workshop comments and discussion-board responses are a different register and
  are not represented.
- **No student text.** Every move was abstracted from a note about a specific piece, so
  the *evidence* for each move lives in a document this corpus deliberately does not
  quote. Anyone auditing this has to go back to the source in Drive.
- **The four freedoms table is my reading of the arcade, not a measurement.** No gate
  tests for freedom of experiment.
