## What Table Four teaches, and where it stops

Keeps:
- Serving clears the loud thing; noticing does not. The end line — every plate went out, that was never the game — is the right thesis.
- The recurring moment unlocks only if you noticed Table 4 twice. That is real hospitality: anticipation built from prior attention.

Stops:
- **The cascade is scripted, not systemic.** Eight authored beats; an unserved beat "forges a link," but the link never changes what happens next. The book's claim is that the room is a connected system. The game only narrates that claim.
- **Noticing is reading.** You notice by tapping a button and reading a sentence. The book's noticing is watching: a coat over the shoulders, a wallet on the table, a gait that says restroom.
- **One seat.** The book is built on three perspectives — guest, server, host. Table Four plays only the server.
- **One night.** "Stranger to regular" is the book's emotional spine, and it happens across visits.
- Debt still open: instruction wall (queue row 62), 50% image floor, token roles, nested-p stamp.

## The games

### 1. The Ticket — RECOMMENDED NEXT
Sentence: *The player hears a table order out loud and writes the kitchen ticket; the kitchen cooks exactly what the ticket says, and the player notices that what they meant and what they wrote are two different things.*
- Book: Front vs. Back of the House; Reading the Ticket; The GPS of Dining (position numbers); Medium or Medium Rare (the grey-burger regular).
- Verb: build a ticket from chips (table, position, item, temp, sub, on the side, fire/hold). No typing.
- The turn: plates come out of the window exactly as written. Double spinach. A steak that is both "medium" and "pink." Fries to position 3 when position 1 ordered them. The guest reacts. Then the player rewrites the ticket.
- Learning: writing for a reader who cannot ask back — audience, precision, register. The kitchen is a literal reader, like a checklist, a compiler, or an AI model. That is the same skill as writing a clear prompt, which makes this a quiet AI-literacy game with no AI in it.
- Audience: MassBay Hospitality Management (A.S. and certificate); EN courses on audience and technical writing; the book's general reader.
- Art: the pass (the window) is the stage — big plates under heat lamps, >50% of the screen. Stage-scale evidence (Mamet): the wrong plate is the size of the screen, not a line of text.
- Reuse: Asking Room card engine for chip-building; Row K staging for the plate reveal.
- Win state: a table that eats without anyone going back to the kitchen.

### 2. Edgar's Spot — answers Waller veto 2
Sentence: *The player works twelve Thursdays across a season with a small cast of recurring guests, and notices that remembering one detail from last visit turns a table number into a regular.*
- Book: A Familiar Face (Alan at 5:30, Trudy's red and sparkling water, Rick's coffee, Edie and Hal never in the dining room); the Edgar's Spot snapshot; The Usual (never say the word); The Recurring Moment; Relationships: Don't Assume.
- Verb: before each night, pre-set the room from memory (hold a salmon off the count, save a stool, bread plates on the high-top). No roster on screen — the player recalls.
- The turn: the cost of a missed moment lands **next visit**, not tonight. The guest does not complain. They just come less.
- Learning: spaced retrieval is the mechanic, not a bolt-on — the player is practicing recall at expanding gaps (Horvath seat). Relationship-building as an accumulation of small, correct memories. The Don't Assume beats keep it honest: a wrong assumption costs more than a blank.
- Payoff: the plaque. Only one guest ever gets one.
- Risk: memory load must be low per night (3–5 new facts), or it becomes a test.

### 3. The Book — answers Waller veto 1
Sentence: *The player builds Saturday's reservation book on the Wallers' own grid, then watches the night arrive, and notices that the pace of the book decides the night before any guest walks in.*
- Book: Reservations — An Introduction (the spreadsheet); How Online Reservations Really Work; Large Parties vs. Small (a twelve-top is six deuces); Nine Ways Reservations Can Go Wrong; What You Can and Can't Control.
- Verb: place parties on a time strip (not a spreadsheet — see RP note); the kitchen's covers-per-slot line glows when crossed. Then play the night: event cards from the Nine Ways arrive and the player applies a remedy.
- Learning: capacity, pacing, queueing, buffer — the core of any service operation, clinic, or classroom schedule. Structure causes behavior (the Beer Game lesson): the "slow kitchen" at 7:30 was booked at 3 p.m.
- RP note: a 20-table × 24-slot grid is visual clutter. Draw the room, not the sheet: tables as places, a single time scrubber, totals as one big number.

### 4. Three Chairs — the reader's game
Sentence: *The player lives the same twenty minutes three times — as host, server, and guest at one table — and notices that each seat saw something the others could not.*
- Book: Homage to a Table (the three monodramas, the airport, the candle); An Unscripted Narrative (the server as narrator who is also a character); Everything's Connected.
- Verb: play the host's seat, then the server's, then sit as the guest; the guest seat reveals the airport ticket, the reason for the corner table, the candle nobody asked for.
- Learning: perspective-taking; the hidden system behind a "lucky" evening. Same flip-seat reveal as Flok.
- Fit: this is the book's own design (tri-perspective), and the most natural companion for readers and for the publisher. Possible book-launch or bookstore piece — permission scope first.

### 5. In the Window — kitchen timing
Sentence: *The player fires items across three stations so a table's plates land in the window together, and notices that the slowest dish sets the whole table's clock.*
- Book: How Long?; Critical Path Fare; Coordination; What You Can and Can't Control (the grill buried, the idle garde manger); Circumventing the System (ordering app and entree together makes it slower).
- Verb: hold-to-fire on a timeline; all-day counts; steer a guest to the idle station.
- Learning: critical-path scheduling, backward planning — project management with steam on it.
- Warning: this is Overcooked's territory. Keep it a planning game (set fire times, then watch), not a reflex game. The book says fast food is not good service.

### 6. Not So Much — the till
Sentence: *The player runs a season's books, choosing charges and comforts, and notices that the covers that looked like a great night become Adjusted Real Covers once the regulars stop coming.*
- Book: Not So Much (ARC/ARR); 30/30/30/10; The Cost of Ambience ($3 × 20 tables × 300 nights); Bottom of the Menu (the substitution charge the Wallers dropped the next day); Automatic Gratuity; If It's Served, Tip on It.
- Verb: toggle a charge or comfort; see the night's till, then the season's return visits.
- Learning: short-term revenue versus lifetime value; quantitative reasoning on real margins.
- Reuse: Real Cost / Sticker Price engine almost directly. Cheapest build on the slate. Fits the iSLO quantitative-reasoning station.

### 7. Behind You — an ears-first game
Sentence: *The player works the line by ear, answering kitchen calls, and notices that a call only counts when someone says it back.*
- Book: the line talk in Coordination; the safety list (announce your whereabouts, never assume, tell everyone what you are doing).
- Verb: listen and answer ("Heard," "Behind," "86"). Screen shows one large shape per call; the game is playable eyes-closed.
- Learning: closed-loop communication — the practice trauma teams train because it measurably improves task completion. Transfers to fire service (Ben Urwin's calibration trainer), nursing, any team.
- Studio fit: first real brief for the new Studio Ears seat. Voice-first, which the founder wants anyway.
- Risk: offline audio. Speech synthesis works offline on most phones but voices vary; recorded short clips would push file size.

### 8. Before They Ask — HOLD
Sentence: *The player watches a silent dining room and acts before a request is spoken, and notices the line between anticipating and assuming.*
- Book: Nine Ways to Distinguish Hospitality from Service; Body Language (coat over shoulders, wallet on the table, the air-scrawl for the check); Relationships: Don't Assume ("She is not his wife").
- Why hold: its core cue is small body language. That is hostile to RP and expensive in art at the Lumino City bar. If built, every cue must be stage-scale and audio-described — which changes the game. Fold its best beat into Three Chairs instead.

### Table Four v2 — if you keep going with Table Four itself
Make the cascade real. The book lists the connections outright: front and back through the guests; host and kitchen through the book; bartender and server through drink orders; bus staff and host through the next guest; kitchen and bartender through soda; late guests and the next party through the table. Put those edges in a small graph; an unserved demand adds load to its neighbors; the next beat is drawn from the loaded node. Same eight-beat length, different night every time. This is the Beer Game move: the player stops blaming a person and starts seeing a structure.

## What to ignore
- A restaurant tycoon sim that holds all of the above. That is the katamari.
- Reflex time-management games (Diner Dash, Overcooked copies). They teach speed; the book argues speed is not service.
- Trivia on wine, bourbon, beer, tipping percentages. That is reference content, not a system. Put it in a glossary card if anywhere.
- The futures chapter (servulants, paid reservations, cannabis). Interesting, not playable yet.
