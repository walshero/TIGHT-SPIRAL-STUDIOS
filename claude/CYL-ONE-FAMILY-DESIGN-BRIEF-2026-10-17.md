# CYL: ONE FAMILY, SIXTY YEARS. Design brief (no build)

**Date:** 2026-10-17 · **Status:** PROPOSAL, for founder review. No code written.
**Asked for (founder, by voice, 2026-10-17):** one family across sixty years; a blind voting beat (quotes without names, the booth, the real result, then who said what); the vote never changes history; and, done thoughtfully and with grounded sources, how the family's own choices shape their household and world. Written through three lenses: Saunders (storytelling), Hitchcock (visual scene coherence), Horvath (choice).
**Builds on canon, does not replace it:** the ratified sentence (`convening/CYL-CONVENING.md`), the drift ruling (`cyl-v7-founder-ruling-2026-08-08.md`), the three-homes brief (`CYL-THREE-HOMES-BRIEF.md`), the shipped v7.

> SENTENCE (founder, ratified 2026-09-18): *The player notices their household in a new light with each address, and notices what wealth and power — through politics — did to where they stand.*

---

## 1. The problem this solves
v7 has six strong rooms that do not touch each other. The design panel's standing criticism is weak integration (Zimmerman: scenes don't alter each other), and playing it on 2026-10-17 confirmed it: nothing chosen in 1962 is still in the room in 2022. The founder: "we're not really succeeding with Choose Your Leader as a full game."

**The fix in one line:** one household, six ballots, six addresses, and a house that keeps a ledger.

## 2. The spine: an election comes before every address
Each of v7's six addresses follows an election that the family lived through:

| Ballot (blind) | Then the address, in the same room | The kid's age (born 1954) | Who votes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1960 | Kennedy, Oct 22 1962 | 6 → 8 | A parent; the kid watches |
| 1964 | Johnson, Aug 4 1964 (the address first; this ballot follows it) | 10 | A parent; "Who would you pick?" |
| 1968 (three candidates) | Nixon, Nov 3 1969 | 14 → 15 | A parent |
| 2012 | Obama, May 23 2013 | 58 → 59 | The kid, now a grandparent |
| 2016 | Trump, Nov 29 2017 | 62 → 63 | The kid |
| 2020 | Biden, Aug 24 2022 | 66 → 68 | The kid, a grandchild in the room |

So every chapter runs: **read blind → vote → the real result → who said what → live with the winner's address in your room.** Johnson is the one exception: he took office by succession, so his address comes first and the ballot after. That is true to history, and it gives the player one ballot where they have already heard one candidate speak.

**What the vote is:** the player's read, recorded. History does not bend. The reveal shows the real national winner and then, quote by quote, who said what.

## 3. The voting beat
- **Booth, not menu.** A curtain, a lever machine (1960s) or a paper ballot (2010s). One thing on screen: the quotes.
- **Quotes are matched for fairness:**
  - same topic: the household economy (work, prices, homes, the draft);
  - same venue type: nomination acceptance speeches, or the same debate;
  - similar length, with names, places, party words and self-references removed.
  - Verbatim, from the American Presidency Project (UCSB) transcripts.
- **Famous lines are excluded.** A line that gives away its speaker turns the blind read into a quiz. Rule: if a cold reader can name the speaker, swap the line. That is the Stranger seat's test, run before the build.
- **Even-handedness binds** (drift ruling, order 8): every ballot gets identical treatment, and the game never scores a party or a vote as right.
- **Abstaining is a real choice** (Osterweil's refusal case). Turnout in these elections was around 55 to 63 percent, so not voting is ordinary. The house records it like any other choice.
- **Audio** (the founder asked for it):
  - Speeches given as official acts by sitting presidents are federal works and generally public domain.
  - Campaign speeches by candidates are not federal works, and archive audio carries its own rights.
  - **Recommendation:** v1 is text on period media: a sample ballot, a newspaper column, a radio transcript card. Add audio clip by clip, only after a rights check. Never use a read-alike voice.

## 4. Choices that shape the house: the ledger
This is the part no iteration has tried. The rule that keeps it honest:

**Everything that changes in the house comes from one of three strands, and the game keeps them visibly separate:**

| Strand | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| CHOICE | What this family did | Took the union job; moved before the kids turned 13 |
| POLICY | What was enacted, dated and sourced, by the winner and Congress | Fair Housing Act 1968; the 1969 draft lottery |
| CHANCE | What nobody chose | A lottery number; a plant closing |

The game never computes a dollar outcome from one choice. It says what research finds for *families like this one*, with the source on the card. That is how a teaching game handles causation without lying about it.

### The four family forks
One per chapter break; each is one decision on one screen.

**F1, 1964. Buy in the new subdivision, or keep renting in the city.**
- *Research:* homeownership is the main way middle-class American households built wealth. The 1930s federal "redlining" maps caused lower homeownership, house values and rents, and more racial segregation, in later decades (Aaronson, Hartley & Mazumder, Chicago Fed / *AEJ: Economic Policy*).
- *Honest limit:* for many families this choice was not offered. If the household is the city apartment reading the Black press (three-homes canon), the subdivision button is there and greyed. **The choice that was not offered is the content.**
- *POLICY strand:* the Fair Housing Act of 1968 arrives in the next chapter.

**F2, 1969. The older brother (born 1950): college, which still carried a student deferment, or the plant.** Then the CHANCE strand: the first draft lottery, held on television on December 1, 1969, draws his birthday.
- *Research:* white Vietnam-era veterans earned about 15 percent less than comparable nonveterans in the early 1980s (Angrist, *American Economic Review*, 1990, Social Security records).
- *Honest limit:* that estimate is for white veterans. The game says so and does not apply it to every family.

**F3, about 1979 (bridge). The kid's first real job: the union job at the plant, or the nonunion office job.**
- *Research:* across the century, union households earned a steady premium of about 10 to 20 log points, and unions reduced inequality (Farber, Herbst, Kuziemko & Naidu, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2021).
- *CHANCE / POLICY strand that follows:* the plant's future. Union density fell for decades; the game shows that as the world changing around a choice that was sound when made.

**F4, about 1995 (bridge). Move the grandkids to a lower-poverty neighborhood, or stay near family.**
- *Research:* in the Moving to Opportunity experiment, children who moved before age 13 earned 31 percent more in their mid-twenties; for children who moved older, effects were if anything negative (Chetty, Hendren & Katz, *American Economic Review*, 2016).
- *Honest limit:* the experiment studied voucher moves out of high-poverty public housing, so the card says which families it describes. The age cutoff becomes the decision's real tension: move now, while it still helps, and leave the grandparents.

### The ending: the braid
In 2022 the house is decomposed in front of the player into its three strands: what the family did, what was enacted, and what was luck. The final frame sets the grandchild's odds against the kid's.
- *Research:* about 90 percent of Americans born in 1940 out-earned their parents; about 50 percent of those born in the 1980s did. Most of the fall comes from growth being shared more unequally, not from slower growth (Chetty et al., *Science*, 2017).
- That is the ratified sentence made playable: wealth and power, through politics, did something to where this family stands, and the player can see which part was theirs.

**Backbone for plausibility:** the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (University of Michigan) has followed real American families and their descendants since 1968. Household trajectories in the game should sit inside what PSID families actually did, and the studio should say so in the credits.

## 5. Three lenses

### Saunders: storytelling
- **The kid is the point of view, and grows up.** The player's first ballot is read over a parent's shoulder at age 6; the last is cast at 66 with a grandchild watching. Escalation is built into a life.
- **Weight arrives sideways, through objects.** One kitchen table carries it all:
  - 1960: the sample ballot;
  - 1969: the lottery numbers in pencil;
  - 1979: the union card;
  - 2013: the refinance papers.
  Nobody says what the table means. By 2022 it does not need saying.
- **Withholding, then the turn.** Who said what is held back until after the vote and the result. The reveal is the record turning on the player's own read (v7's delta, kept), not a lesson.
- **No narrator verdicts.** The family does not explain itself; dinner arguments are heard through a doorway.

### Hitchcock: visual scene coherence
- **One fixed camera on one room for sixty years.** What ages, appears and goes missing in the frame is the story: the TV set changes, the wallpaper fades, a chair stays. The room is the save file.
- **Three-frame board per chapter:** the room (receiver point of view) → the booth (curtain closed, private) → the reveal (match cut from the ballot to the result to the room).
- **The bomb under the table** is the player's sealed ballot. It sits visibly in the room, on the table, through the result and the reveal, so the audience knows something the player has not checked yet.
- **Never a face** (period bible, binding):
  - candidates are words and voices only;
  - family members are hands, backs and doorways;
  - the photographic-room art lane is mounted, not reinvented.

### Horvath: choice
- **Attribution is the lesson.** The near miss to guard against: the player credits or blames the president for what was the family's choice or chance, or blames the family for what was policy. The braid makes attribution checkable at every chapter's end.
- **Load: one decision per screen.** The vote and the family fork never share a screen. Each chapter asks for at most two decisions.
- **The delta instrument stays:** the blind read beside the reveal, one glance away, at every altitude (Coordinator seat, 2026-09-17).
- **Refusal plays** (Osterweil): abstain, never move, never take the union job. The house renders the result as its own legible world, never a scold.

## 6. Structure and length
- **Chapter 1, The Cold War Kitchen:** ballots 1960, 1964 and 1968; three addresses; forks F1 and F2. About 12 minutes.
- **Bridge, The Years Between:** forks F3 and F4, two screens each, the room aging across the cut.
- **Chapter 2, The Same Kitchen:** ballots 2012, 2016 and 2020; three addresses; the braid. About 12 minutes.
- **Resume between chapters** answers the 20-minute problem the Coordinator named. v7 promises "no storage ever", so resuming would use a short chapter code the player writes down, not saved data. Founder call.

## 7. Founder decisions before any build
1. **Which household?** One family cannot be all three homes. Recommendation: the player picks one of the three homes at the start. The three-homes canon then becomes the replay structure: same sixty years, different doors open.
2. **Quote sourcing:** approve the fairness rules in §3, and confirm the Trump and Biden ballots use the same rules as the others.
3. **Audio:** text-first for v1 (recommended), or rights-check audio now.
4. **The forks:** keep, cut or replace any of F1 to F4. The F1 race limit in particular should be his call on how it is shown.
5. **Copy:** all family dialogue is studio placeholder until the founder writes or approves it (drift ruling, order 2).

## 8. Build plan once approved
1. **First slice: Chapter 1 only, one household.** Ballot 1960 → 1962 address → F1 → 1964 address and ballot → 1968 ballot → 1969 address → F2 and the lottery.
2. **Pipeline before the playtest link:** routes in `tsp-routes.json` (vote, abstain, refuse every fork), the playthrough agent, Studio Eyes, Studio Fingers, `senses-run.sh` and the senses seats, fixes, then the playtest site.
3. **The braid ships last,** after Chapter 2 exists, because it needs both chapters to mean anything.

**Ignore for now:** audio, the full sixty years in one build, any scoring of votes.
