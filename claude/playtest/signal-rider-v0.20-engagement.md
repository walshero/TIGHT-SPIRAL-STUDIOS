# Signal Rider v0.20: Engagement seat

Played the real build at 390x844 in headless Chrome, steered by a bot that reads game state through a hook in a copy of the file (/home/claude/sr-work/sr-hook.html; the build itself was not touched). Runs: the default week (warm-up, one full ride with a fade and a checkpoint return, the end card, the start of ride 2) and an all-stress week (two fades, two checkpoint returns). Shots are in /home/claude/sr-table/engagement/; logs are /home/claude/sr-work/clean.log and stress.log. Ride timings are upper bounds, because two browsers shared the machine and the game caps its frame step at 0.05 s.

## 1. What works (keep)

- Scene first, one instruction. At 0.2 s the tube is moving and the only text is "Hold the left or right side to steer" (good-00-open-0.2s.png). A cold newcomer gets it within the 3-second target.
- The warm-up teaches by doing. You steer into one green, then one red, and the picture card sits in the top bar, clear of the play area (warm-9.9.png).
- The lock-and-key landing is the best-looking moment in the game. You get a big key shape, receptor shapes on the spines, and a Match / Wrong shape / Missed verdict in under a second (clean-014.png). It gives clear competence feedback (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006).
- Failing costs little. Back to the checkpoint is one tap and puts you back about 30 units earlier with hits reset. Juul (2013, *The Art of Failure*) argues failure keeps players coming back when it is cheap and points toward getting better.
- "Try next: add a block from Skills" on the end and fade cards. It answers "where to next," the most useful kind of feedback (Hattie & Timperley 2007).
- The week blocks come from a community college student's real life ("Closing shift, then class", "Rent due Friday", "Missed the bus, in the rain"). These are evocative objects in Turkle's sense (2007): the player sees their own week in the tube. Keep the wording.

## 2. Top findings, ranked

**F1. One mistake at a red wall can cost two of your three hits.** Evidence: the stress log shows "Loud, crowded room" at s=323.1 and "Missed the bus" at s=323.6: hits 2 and 3 from one wall, under a second apart, then the fade (stress.log; stress-sheet-sFade-24.png). It happened again at s=553.9 and s=554.6. Code: red walls space blocks 0.5 rad apart (line ~258, `a0+(k-(n-1)/2)*0.5`), and the hit test is `<0.42` rad (line ~990). Because 0.84 is more than 0.5, about two-thirds of the space between neighbouring blocks hits both. Stressed weeks make walls one block bigger, so they are hit hardest. Why it matters: the player sees one bump and loses two hearts. That breaks the cause-and-effect link that competence depends on (Ryan et al. 2006), and it undercuts the SAMHSA (2014) safety principle. The game feels unfair rather than hard. Fix: count at most one hit per group (`grp` already exists). Still show and name the second block's picture, and log it for the end card. Optionally narrow the hit radius to 0.3 so a gap you can see is a gap you can use. Size S.

**F2. Ride 2 is the same as ride 1, with no score to beat.** Evidence: the end card leads with "You reached the thinking cortex." The one real score, "about 17.7 milliseconds", is buried in a sentence (clean-sheet-sEnd-52.png). Nothing carries over between rides (buildRun rebuilds everything; the only localStorage key is comfort settings). In ride 2 the same lesson toasts and speech play again ("Bright rings are gaps..." at s=238 in both rides, clean.log). Why it matters: the travel time is a mastery measure that already fits the science, because greens speed the signal and so cut milliseconds. Players aiming to beat their own last ride is the self-referenced mastery goal Ames (1992) links to persistence. Repeating guidance to players who have already absorbed it lowers learning and engagement (expertise reversal effect, Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003). Fix: make "Your touch arrived in 17.7 ms" the headline. Add "Last ride 19.2 ms" and "Best this visit 16.8 ms", held in memory only, nothing saved, so the "Nothing is saved" promise holds. From ride 2 on, skip lesson toasts the player has already seen this visit. Size S.

**F3. "Try next" tells you what to add but makes you go find it.** Evidence: the end card's next step is a sentence; the buttons are "Change the week" (the full tray, with Ride about 1730 px down) and "Ride the same week." Why it matters: McGonigal (2011) names a clear goal and a feedback system as core traits of a game; here the goal is clear but acting on it takes several steps. Fix: one button, "Add a Skills block and ride", that adds the block, shows the panel's preview change for a beat, then starts the ride. This is the "change one thing, ride again" loop that drives rides 2 and 3. Size S.

**F4. The pre-ride inventory reads as a wall of text.** Evidence: the tray is 232 words. The intro paragraph alone is 55 words before any choice, and "Ride this week" sits 1730 px down in a 606 px panel, about 2.8 screens of scrolling (good-tray.png; measured). Each category adds a research paragraph and an "In the game" line (tray-cat1.png). Why it matters: the founder bars opening walls, and Rigby & Ryan (2011) tie autonomy to choices that are meaningful and quick to make. The choices here are meaningful, but they are slow to reach. Fix, within the v0.14 guided-path ruling: cut the intro paragraph, since the live balance and spine preview already show what the week does. Collapse each research line behind a "Why?" toggle. Keep the categories, blocks and preview. Size S. UNPROVEN that this changes how many players reach Ride; it needs a human playtest.

**F5. Greens all sound alike, and the default week shows only two kinds.** Evidence: the default week gave "A friend who listens x6, A walk or workout x7" in one ride (clean.log end card). Each green plays the same sfxGood and speaks its name every time, and greens laid out as a line to "ride along" give no sign that you are chaining them. Why it matters: "Juice it or lose it" (Jonasson & Purho, GDC Europe 2012) shows how layered, amplified response to each player action makes the same mechanic feel alive. Thirteen identical chimes and spoken names wear thin. Fix: raise the pitch a step for each green caught in the same line, with a brighter forest flash on the last one. Speak a green's name only the first time it appears in a ride. Seed the default week with three support categories so the rotation has range. Size S.

**F6. The first decision you actually make arrives late.** Evidence: the first green sits at s=30, reached at about 9.9 s after load (single-browser run). The first landing puzzle, the game's most distinctive mechanic, arrives at s=230, roughly 45 s or more into the ride (ramp math, line 956; clean.log at s=230.1). The warm-up has no landing at all (`!run.warm` guard, line 971). Why it matters: a cold newcomer needs a meaningful action within seconds, and the most novel verb should be met early, while curiosity is high. Fix: move the first warm-up green to about s=12, so the first decision comes around 4 s. Add a one-beat, no-fail lock-and-key landing at the end of the warm-up. Size M. UNPROVEN on attention; inferred from timing.

## 3. The one change I'd make first

F1: one hit per red group. It is a single-condition fix, and it removes the most unfair moment in the game. It matters most for the stressed weeks the game exists to make survivable.
