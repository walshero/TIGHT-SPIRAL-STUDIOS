FOUNDER: "I want Todd Gitlin, Scot Osterweil, sherry turtle, and college professors teaching
critical thinking and political speech. Have them playtest and advise"

Build played: `lab/dunk-o-rama.html` v0.6 (md5 66983b29), at the founder's measured phone size
(263 x 480 CSS px) and at 390 x 844. Bench run inline, one pass (CLAUDE.md cost discipline).

**These are lenses, not voices.** Each seat reasons from the person's published work. Nothing
here is their words or their endorsement. Todd Gitlin died in 2022; his seat works only from
what he wrote. Scot Osterweil is the studio's first board adviser in life; this seat is a
stand-in until he plays it himself.

## SEAT: Todd Gitlin (media, framing, spectacle)

Lens: *The Whole World Is Watching* (1980) on framing (selection, emphasis and exclusion), and
*Media Unlimited* (2001) on the flood of media images and the feelings they sell.

- **The crowd's questions are frames too, and the game treats them as innocent.** Every crowd
  line is fair, plain and right. Real crowds, and real questioners, select and emphasize. The
  sharper lesson is that framing starts before anyone cheats. Recommend: at Level 5 or a new
  level, one in four crowd questions is itself loaded ("Why does the mayor waste our money?"),
  and the player can call the crowd.
- **The dunk is spectacle, and spectacle rewards contempt.** The pleasure loop is humiliating a
  politician. That is the carnival's honest joy, and it is also the media habit Gitlin warned
  about: politics as a show where the payoff is someone getting wet. Keep it, but let the game
  notice it once: a politician who answers fairly ("I don't know yet; here is what I'd check")
  and a reaction if you dunk them anyway.
- **Watch the cynicism tax.** Twenty cheats and no honest answer teaches that all public speech
  is fraud. Cynicism is not critical thinking; it is its lazy cousin.

## SEAT: Scot Osterweil (play, the four freedoms)

Lens: the four freedoms of play (to fail, to experiment, to fashion identities, of effort), from
Klopfer, Osterweil and Salen, *Moving Learning Games Forward* (2009).

- **Freedom of effort: good.** Calling is optional and the throw never waits. Keep it.
- **Freedom to fail: good.** A wrong call costs only the bonus.
- **The call is a quiz with a nice reward.** Three buttons under a text card is a worksheet
  wearing a carnival hat. The fun is in the throw; the learning sits beside it. The fix is not
  more text. Make the identification physical: the player throws at a cheat, not at a button.
  One version: three signs swing on the target frame, each naming a move; hit the right sign to
  call it.
- **Freedom to fashion identities is missing.** You are only ever the thrower. Put the player
  on the seat: they build a heckle from parts and the crowd throws at them (the Heckle Bench,
  D4). Writing the cheat is how you learn to hear it.
- **Too much text.** His standing note to this studio holds: at the founder's size, three of
  every four cards scroll. Cut every exchange to the joke.

## SEAT: Sherry Turkle (conversation, empathy, attention)

Lens: *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015) and *Alone Together* (2011).

- **Fallacy-spotting can become a way to stop listening.** A player who learns "that's a straw
  man!" can use it to dismiss a person instead of answering them. The game rewards naming, then
  throwing. Nothing rewards the harder move: answering fairly.
- **The fair version is buried.** It appears only on the round card. Put it in play: after a
  correct call, the player picks the fair way to say it from two options, and the politician
  says it back. That turns a gotcha into a repair.
- **Give the crowd a person.** "Crowd" is a faceless mob. One named regular (the crossing guard,
  the twelve-year-old who found the lake pollution) who asks every question would make the
  exchange a conversation between people rather than a hit list.
- **Solitude before talk.** Three reading-heavy decisions every eight seconds leaves no room to
  think. One quiet beat per round (the round card already does this) is enough. Do not add more
  timers.

## SEAT: Professor of Rhetoric and Composition (argument units, first-year writing)

Lens: classroom practice with *They Say / I Say* (Graff and Birkenstein) and Toulmin-style
argument. This is the course where the game would most likely live (D5).

- **Students need the claim, then the move.** The two-voice exchange does this well: it is the
  "they say" before the response. Keep it.
- **Names are cheap, transfer is not.** Students can label twenty fallacies and still miss them
  in a real op-ed. Add one transfer step: a closing card with a fresh, unlabeled line and an
  open question ("What would you ask back?"), not a multiple choice.
- **Classroom use needs a teacher sheet**: the 20 moves, the 60 exchanges, and a 10-minute
  discussion plan. One page, printable from the game.

## SEAT: Professor of Political Communication (deception, ads, framing)

Lens: Kathleen Hall Jamieson's *Dirty Politics: Deception, Distraction, and Democracy* (1992) on
how political messages mislead by distraction as much as by lies.

- **Distraction is the master move, and the Filibusterer already teaches it.** Lead with it:
  Level 1 is right.
- **The Pundit is the most realistic level and the most dangerous.** Scapegoating and loaded
  questions are where real harm happens. Keep the targets absurd (pigeons, the marching band),
  never a group of people. Every current line passes that test; hold it for new lines.
- **Even-handedness holds.** No line maps onto a party. Keep the town-issue rule absolute.

## SEAT: Professor of Critical Thinking (informal logic)

Lens: Boudry, Paglieri and Pigliucci, "The Fake, the Flimsy, and the Fallacious" (*Argumentation*,
2015), which argues that many named fallacies are only fallacious in context.

- **The game risks the "fallacy fallacy."** Appeal to tradition, appeal to authority and the
  slippery slope are sometimes good arguments. A real engineer on a bridge is a fine authority.
  The field guide's "fair version" lines start to say this; the play does not.
- **Add the honest twin.** For a few moves, include an exchange where the same form is used
  fairly ("The state engineer, who inspects bridges, says it needs repair"). The right call is
  "No cheat." That one button would teach more judgment than five more fallacies.

## PANEL VERDICT

The mechanic works: calling the move steadies the throw, and the two-voice exchange makes the
cheat visible. The panel's shared worry is the lesson underneath: every politician cheats, the
crowd is always right, and naming a fallacy ends the conversation. That is cynicism with a
vocabulary, not critical thinking.

## RECOMMENDATIONS, IN ORDER (founder rules)

1. **Fix the hint-on-glove defect** (Fingers). It can make the founder's first throw fail.
   Studio has this ready to fix in v0.7.
2. **Add "No cheat"** (Critical Thinking, Gitlin, Turkle): about one exchange in five is a fair
   answer or a fair use of the same form, and "No cheat" joins the choices. Dunking an honest
   answer draws a reaction from the crowd.
3. **Cut every exchange to fit the card at 263 x 480** (Osterweil): target under 18 words, no
   scrolling. Today 45 of 60 scroll.
4. **Fair version in play** (Turkle): after a right call, pick the fair way to say it; the
   politician says it back.
5. **The crowd can cheat too** (Gitlin): a named regular asks the questions, and sometimes the
   question is the loaded one.
6. **Physical calling** (Osterweil): hit the sign that names the move. Larger build; after 2 to 5.
7. **Transfer card and teacher sheet** (Rhetoric): one unlabeled line per round with an open
   question; a printable one-page guide.

Recommended first build (v0.7): 1, 2 and 3 together. Why: they fix a defect, answer the panel's
main worry, and remove most of the reading load, all without new screens. Trade-off: about 15
new fair exchanges to write and every existing exchange trimmed. Simpler: 1 and 3 only. More
ambitious: 1 to 5, which turns the game from "spot the cheat" into "spot it, then fix it."
