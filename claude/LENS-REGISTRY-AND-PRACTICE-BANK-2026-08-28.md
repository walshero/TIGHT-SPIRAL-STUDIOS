# LENS REGISTRY + PRACTICE BANK
### the "Read as X" instructions, mined from all four workshops, with excerpts to practice on

**2026-08-28.** Founder: *"I have workshop options in summer 2026 and spring 2026. Mine
them. Several workshops have Read as XYZ instructions. Let's preload some practice pieces
(short excerpts) to practice craft moves ID and critique for impact."*

This answers the gap the corpus named: **freedom to fashion identities, absent from the

the reader a role and says *read as that*. Nobody has to invent an identity layer; it

## PART A: THE LENS REGISTRY

### A0 · THE READER ROLES, which are the actual "Read as X" instructions

**Correction, and worth recording.** The first cut of this file mined the four workshop
instruction documents and reported that the lenses lived there. Founder: *"Lenses live in
Google Docs. See course schedule and docs with instructions. No forms."* He is right. The
named roles are in the **Course Schedule**, in Workshop 4, and they are sharper than
anything in the instruction docs. I looked in the instruction files and the Forms and
stopped before the schedule, which is the document the syllabus links first.

**The three roles, verbatim:**

> **Reader Roles**
> Each pass, take one of these three reader roles. Use a different role on each of your
> first three pieces.
>
> 1. **The Compression Reader**: Where does the piece earn its brevity, and where could it
>    cut? Look for the line that does the work of three, the wind-up before the real first
>    sentence, the explaining the scene already shows.
> 2. **The Image Reader**: What can you see, hear, and touch, and where does it blur? Look
>    for the concrete detail that lands, the named feeling that should be shown instead,
>    the abstraction with no picture under it.
> 3. **The Turn Reader**: Does the ending arrive late and quiet, or does it just stop? Look
>    for where the story turns, whether the last line lands sideways on an object or
>    gesture, whether the close is earned by what came before.

**These are proper roles, not focus areas.** They have names, they are taken one at a
time, and they rotate. Each carries three specific things to hunt for. That is a deck of
cards, already written.

The fourth piece in Workshop 4 is not a response to the writing at all:

**Thirteen lenses across four workshops**, plus the three named roles above.** All are the founder's own, from the current
instruction documents. The shape is identical every time and is worth naming, because it
is a reusable game structure:

> **Choose ONE as your primary lens. You may briefly draw from the others. Try to switch
> approaches from one piece to the next.**

That single rule is the whole identity mechanic. You do not read as yourself; you take a
seat, you stay in it, and you take a different seat next round.

### A1 · CREATIVE NONFICTION (Workshop 1): four focus questions, not named lenses

| # | lens | the question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Immersion** | Can you visualize the setting, feel the stakes, hear the narrator? |
| 2 | **Language** | Specific and sensory? Or vague and cliché? |
| 3 | **Structure & Focus** | Does organization support the emotional arc? What drifts or feels "extra"? |
| 4 | **Needs of the Reader** | Where were you disoriented? What is missing? |

*Note: CNF is the only workshop without named first-person lenses. It has focus questions
instead. **Design opportunity:** it is the outlier, and the other three suggest what its
lenses would be.*

### A2 · POETRY (Workshop 2): three lenses

| # | lens | read as |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **THE READER EXPERIENCE** | someone reporting what stayed with them |
| 2 | **READING FOR CRAFT** | someone tracing how the effect was made |
| 3 | **READING THROUGH QUESTIONS** | someone curious, holding possibility open |

Sentence starters, verbatim: *"The line that stayed with me most was..." · "I could
clearly picture..." · "I noticed several images related to..." · "The repetition of ___
helped create..." · "The poem seemed to shift when..." · "I found myself wondering..." ·
"I'd like to know more about..."*

### A3 · THE 10-MINUTE PLAY (Workshop 3): three lenses, one of them split

| # | lens | read as |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **THE AUDIENCE EXPERIENCE** | *"someone watching the play, not just reading it"* |
| 2 | **READING FOR TENSION & STAKES** | someone tracking what is versus what |
| 3 | **READING FOR DIALOGUE & STAGECRAFT** | choose: dialogue, or stage directions, or both |

**This workshop is the clearest "read as" instruction in the course, and it has a second
identity built in:** the reader occupies two seats at once.

> *"Your job is to help the writer understand how their play is landing with a thoughtful
> reader **and with an imagined audience.** That's you, in both seats."*

And the reason, which is a game mechanic waiting to happen:

> *"A reader holds the whole script at once, but an audience only ever has the present
> moment. They don't know that the At Rise says 'Ernesto has claustrophobia,' but the
> actors and directors and stage managers and lighting and sound all do."*

Also here, from Sellers: *"Happy Man + All Desires Fulfilled = Boring. No tension."*

The schedule additionally assigns **Responding to Plays and Screenplays**, seven questions
from Sellers: the story of the scene; the psychology of the people; conflicts in the
dialogue; conflicts in the action; conflicts in the setting; what changes over the play;
and *"What images do you see in your mind as you listen to the dialogue?"*

### A4 · FLASH FICTION (Workshop 4): four lenses, and the most developed set in the course

Each lens carries prompts, sentence starters, **and an "If it's not landing, try…" repair
move.** That last part is the missing half of every critique game: it tells the responder
what to *offer*, not just what to notice.

| # | lens | the repair move it hands you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A WORLD YOU CAN SEE** | point at one vague stretch, ask for a single concrete detail. *"In flash, one sharp image beats five fuzzy ones."* |
| 2 | **SOMEONE WHO WANTS SOMETHING** | name the visible want, ask whether a quieter want hides beneath. *"The gap between the two is where a short piece gets its pressure."* |
| 3 | **A REASON TO LEAN FORWARD** | ask the writer to translate a distant risk into something losable **today** |
| 4 | **A TURN THAT LANDS** | ask whether an early innocuous detail could be planted so the ending **clicks backward** |

And the sharpest diagnostic in the whole course, a test a game can actually run:

> **"Test: if you cut the turn, would the story basically stay the same? If yes, it may
> not be a real turn yet."**

Plus the reading stance for the genre:

> *"if this piece vanished, would it matter? What job would go undone? That question, what
> each moment is doing for the reader, is the heart of how we'll read."*

### A5 · WHAT THE REGISTRY REVEALS

1. **The identity layer already exists and is course-canonical.** Thirteen seats. No
   invention required.
2. **The lenses are uneven in development.** Flash has repair moves; poetry and play have
   sentence starters only; CNF has neither. **A game could carry the whole set at flash's
   level of development**, which would be a real contribution back to the course.
3. **Every workshop ends on the same sentence**, near enough verbatim: *"Writers will
   always make the final call, and you can help make that call more informed."*
4. **Two workshops open with the same disclaimer:** the goal is not to decide what a piece
   *means*, and *"it's certainly not to evaluate whether it is 'good' or 'bad.'"*

## PART B: THE PRACTICE BANK

### B0 · Provenance rules, and why they are what they are

Practice pieces must be **critiqueable**, which finished masterpieces are not. You cannot
practice "if it's not landing, try…" on a poem that lands. So the bank is built from:

| class | what it is | why it is safe |
|---|---|---|
| **STUDIO** | written for this bank, with a named problem planted | no author to wound, no licence, no student |
| **PD** | United States public domain, pre-1931, set exactly | published, out of copyright |
| **FOUNDER** | Matt's own course texts | his, and the text-bank policy already allows it |

**No student work.** Founder ruling 2026-08-10. Every STUDIO piece below is machine-written
for this purpose and has no author on purpose, which is the same logic that makes Cliché
Hunter's plates authorless: you are meant to critique it freely.

Each entry carries: the lens it trains, the moves planted, and **what a strong response
would name** (the answer key for critique-for-impact, never shown before the player tries).

### B1 · CREATIVE NONFICTION

**CNF-01 · STUDIO · lens: Language (vague vs specific)**
> The kitchen was really nice and always smelled amazing. My grandmother would make food
> for everybody, and it meant so much to all of us. Those were good times that I will
> never forget. Family is what matters at the end of the day.

*Planted:* four abstractions in a row; two word packets ("at the end of the day," "never
forget"); zero sensory data.
*Strong response names:* the camera never gets closer than "food." Founder's move applies
directly: this is eating "fruit." Ask for the one dish, the one sound, the one hand.

**CNF-02 · STUDIO · lens: Structure & Focus (the "extra")**
> The bus took forty minutes. I had a window seat. I had packed the night before, three
> shirts and the good shoes, and I checked twice that the letter was in the front pocket.
> There was traffic near the bridge. At the hospital, the letter was still in my pocket
> when I left.

*Planted:* a real spine (the letter) buried under transit logistics that do no work.
*Strong response names:* the letter is the story and it appears twice, in a piece where
"three shirts" and "traffic near the bridge" get equal weight. His 5% Cut, and Slide.

### B2 · POETRY

**POE-01 · STUDIO · lens: Reading for Craft · the universal/distinct stage**
> I am from long summers and short winters,
> from the smell of my mother's cooking,
> from music playing in the other room,
> from love that was always there.

*Planted:* every line true of millions of people. No line is false; none is anyone's.
*Strong response names:* **this nails the universal.** The next move is to invite the
distinct. Which music. Whose room. This is the highest-value note in the founder's corpus
and this excerpt exists to make it visible in four lines.

**POE-02 · STUDIO · lens: Reading for Craft · the line break already pauses**
> The porch light stayed on,
> all night,
> for no one,
> and the moths came anyway.

*Planted:* three commas doing work the line breaks already did.
*Strong response names:* his note exactly. The breaks make the pause; if you constrain the
commas, the one you keep will matter. Try it with none and hear what changes.

**POE-03 · PD · lens: The Reader Experience · a move working**
> The fog comes
> on little cat feet.
>
> It sits looking
> over harbor and city
> on silent haunches
> and then moves on.

*Carl Sandburg, "Fog," Chicago Poems, 1916. United States public domain, set as published.*
*Present:* one metaphor sustained six lines; the animal never named after line 2.
*Strong response names:* what the image does that a sentence could not. Also the
**coined** trap: this reads pre-cast now **because it is the source.** Pairs with Cliché
Hunter.

### B3 · THE 10-MINUTE PLAY

**PLA-01 · STUDIO · lens: Dialogue · on the nose**
> **RAY:** I'm angry at you because you forgot my birthday, and it makes me feel like I
> don't matter to you.
> **MAGGIE:** I know. I've been avoiding you because I feel guilty about it.

*Planted:* both characters state their interior weather aloud. Nothing is left for the
audience.
*Strong response names:* no subtext, no double duty. The founder's fix is structural: let
one of them let something slip, then dig the hole deeper.

**PLA-02 · STUDIO · lens: Stage directions · information the audience never gets**
> *(RAY enters. He has not slept since the funeral, and he has decided tonight is the
> night he tells her.)*
> **RAY:** Cold out.
> **MAGGIE:** Shut the door.

*Planted:* the entire dramatic situation lives in a stage direction. His own words for this
failure: the audience *"don't know that the At Rise says 'Ernesto has claustrophobia.'"*
*Strong response names:* an audience sees a man say "cold out." Everything that matters is
invisible from a seat.

**PLA-03 · STUDIO · lens: Tension & Stakes · the pressure cooker reset**
> **MAGGIE:** Say it. Say the thing you came here to say.
> **RAY:** I…
> *(Blackout. Lights up. Later. They are drinking tea.)*

*Planted:* the scene cuts at maximum pressure and returns after the crisis.
*Strong response names:* his note. *"When things get crazy is when you test your characters
to see what they're made of."* Do not drop the curtain there. Do not let the pressure
cooker reset.

### B4 · FLASH FICTION

**FLA-01 · STUDIO · lens: A Turn That Lands · apply the cut test**
> She checked the mailbox on the way in, the way she had every day for a year. Bills, a
> flyer, nothing. Inside, she made tea and sat by the window until the light went. In the
> morning she checked the mailbox again.

*Planted:* a situation, atmosphere, and **no turn.**
*Strong response names:* run the test. Cut the ending and the story is unchanged, so there
is no real turn yet. Then offer the repair: what early detail could be planted so an
ending would click backward?

**FLA-02 · STUDIO · lens: Someone Who Wants Something · the second want**
> He wanted the promotion. He had wanted it for two years. When they gave it to Dana he
> shook her hand, and he meant it, and he went to the parking garage and sat in his car
> for a while before he drove home.

*Planted:* a clean surface want and a quieter one showing only in behaviour.
*Strong response names:* the surface want is stated in sentence one. The real one is in the
car and never named. **The gap between the two is where the pressure is** and this piece
has it, so this is a piece to praise specifically, not repair.

**FLA-03 · FOUNDER · lens: A World You Can See · and an open question**
> *Station Seven (Mars)*, in Drive, titled "Flash Fiction Model Text, Workshop 4 exemplar."

**Flagged rather than used, and this needs the founder's answer.** The file is labelled a
model exemplar, but on reading it does not behave like one:

- **It is roughly 950 words.** His own flash instructions say the genre works *"with only
  a few hundred words"* and that *"every detail, every line of dialogue, and every silence
  has to earn its place."*
- It runs on stacked abstraction of the kind his feedback consistently flags: *defied every
  logical explanation*, *absolute bewilderment*, *the awe washing over him*, *settling deep
  into his bones*.
- The pressure gauge is introduced as a life-or-death system, rises during the encounter,
  then *"slowly normalized"* with no consequence. The story's one hard stake is dropped.
- A long Earth-memory passage arrives as exposition rather than pressure.

**Two readings, and I will not guess between them.** Either it is a positive exemplar, in
which case it disagrees with his own instructions on length and on earning place; or it is
a **deliberate anti-model**, a specimen of machine-shaped prose for students to diagnose,
which would fit a professor on the AI task force who has been catching LLM submissions.

**If it is the second, it is the single best critique-practice text available**, because it
fails four of his own lenses in ways a student can name. It needs a label either way, since
a file called "Model Text" will be imitated.

**FND-01 · FOUNDER · "China Gold" · lenses: The Image Reader, The Turn Reader**

<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-BEGIN Matt Walsh, "China Gold," reproduced exactly with permission 2026-08-28 -->
> Before it burned down
> I used to go to China Gold with Sophie and my mom
> for fried rice and egg rolls
> under a three-paneled painting
> of fine-ridged mountains rising up from the moss-like trees and the mist —
> and barely discernible on the mountain path: a crouching figure with a walking stick,
> proof of man's existence.
>
> "You can go there," Sophie said, drinking from a tiny patterned cup,
> though I'm not sure I believed her then.
>
> Years later, climbing Cloud Path in the mountains of western Sichuan
> I ascended into the mist with my walking stick
> over a crumbling staircase, past a rain-slick temple,
> and then I saw myself over the chasm, out in the sky,
> my massive twelve-year-old self cracking open a fortune cookie, oblivious,
> and I yelled to me, hands cupped around my mouth,
> all the things that I should know
> all the things about to come...
> but the boy in the booth couldn't hear the man on the mountain.
> The boy just played with toothpicks and chunks of pineapple
> until it was time to pay the bill and go home.
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-END -->

**This is the answer key to his own corpus, in one poem.**

- **The universal and the distinct.** Nobody else's childhood is this one. Not "a
  restaurant" but China Gold, before it burned down; not "food" but fried rice and egg
  rolls; not "a cup" but a tiny patterned cup. Compare **POE-01**, the studio draft built
  to be true of millions. **Same genre, same territory, opposite ends of the move.** That
  pairing is the single best teaching object in the bank.
- **The turn, and it clicks backward.** The painting's crouching figure with a walking
  stick is planted in stanza one as decoration. Years later he *is* that figure, on that
  path, with that stick. His own Turn Reader test: cut the turn and the poem is nothing.
- **The last line lands sideways on an object.** Toothpicks, pineapple, the bill. Exactly
  what the Turn Reader is told to look for.
- **Sophie's line is the hinge and it is dialogue, not statement.** *"You can go there."*
  The poem never explains it. Nothing is on the nose.
- **Ambiguity of feeling, not of event.** You always know what happened. What it costs him
  is left open, which is the distinction he draws and few pieces get right.

*Playable as:* the paired specimen for universal/distinct, and the worked example for the
Turn Reader before a player takes that role.

**FND-02 · FOUNDER · "The Road to Monserrat" · lenses: The Turn Reader, The Compression Reader**

*A prose poem, not nonfiction. Reproduced exactly, including the title's spelling, which
differs from the spelling inside the piece. Not corrected: it is his file.*

<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-BEGIN Matt Walsh, "The Road to Monserrat," reproduced exactly with permission 2026-08-28 -->
> Himmler and the Nazis drove on Montserrat for the Holy Grail, a column of sedans sewing
> up the switchbacks over Catalonia, Wagner's *Parsifal* blaring from a radio. Himmler
> counts the teeth of the mountain spine and dreams of seizing the black madonna.
>
> That morning while the soldiers provisioned, one of Franco's indignant peasants removed
> the spark plugs from the munitions truck. At a seaside vista an hour later. another had
> poured sand in a gas tank. As they ascended the mountain, the entourage braked frequently
> and with polysyllabic curses for the boulders and dead trees that children and
> grandmothers had pushed in front of them.
>
> But here they come now, marching across the esplanade in the October light. The abbot
> happens to be away, so Ripol Noble greets them in German and receives them in the
> monastery square, where it is cool, and the midday sky is white. Here he directs
> Himmler's men to the library, to the black madonna, to the monastery vaults.
>
> There is one monk, let's say Sebastian, who fears for the work he's been transcribing:
> it predates the other manuscripts, and fears that its truth, once seen, will reveal him
> as someone who would share that truth, and so he has eaten the pages.
>
> I think about Sebastian, and suddenly he's in a classroom in Florida, he's the librarian
> there, and the Moms for Liberty are pulling the books from the shelves and into a cart
> with wheels. The books disappear into the cart. The Moms for Liberty pull Sebastian by
> his long robe. They pull him by his long beard, opening his mouth, and Linda has her
> whole arm in up to the elbow and pulls out the pages that he's torn out and swallowed,
> yellow slick with bile like the yolk of an egg, and she drops them into the cart with
> wheels, and pushes the monk, who's leaning in anyway for his pages, into the cart with
> wheels, with the rest of the books, and he is gone, and there is no sound, other than the
> squeak of wheels over the frayed carpet
<!-- SOURCE-VERBATIM-END -->

**What it teaches:**

- **The turn is a hinge between two centuries**, and it is announced by four plain words:
  *"I think about Sebastian."* The writer steps into his own poem and the whole thing
  changes register. A Turn Reader can name the exact word where it happens.
- **The swallowed pages are planted, then paid.** Sebastian eats them in 1940; Linda pulls
  them out in a Florida classroom. Same object, two eras. This is his "click backward,"
  built across a historical gap.
- **The last line lands sideways on an object**, again: the squeak of wheels over frayed
  carpet. No summary, no moral.
- **Compression Reader material.** *"let's say Sebastian"* invents a character in three
  words and never apologises for it. The whole second paragraph is sabotage rendered as a
  list, no scene needed.
- **No closing punctuation.** The piece stops rather than ends. Worth asking a player
  whether that is a turn that lands or a piece that just stops, since that is exactly the
  question the Turn Reader is handed, and this one is arguable.

*Playable as:* the hardest specimen in the bank, and the one where two readers will
disagree. That disagreement is the point: a reading is a position, not a verdict.

## PART C: HOW THIS GETS PLAYED

Not a design. The pieces a design now has in hand.

1. **Deal a seat, not a question.** "You are reading as the audience" is a role, not a
   prompt. Thirteen seats exist; rotate them, per his own switching rule.
2. **The same excerpt under two lenses gives two true answers.** That is the strongest
   argument for a lens game: it proves a reading is a *position*, not a verdict, which is
   the course's own stance.
3. **Critique for impact means producing a response, not selecting one.** His sentence
   starters are scaffolds and his "If it's not landing, try…" notes are the repair half.
   A response built from *starter + specific moment + repair* is the trainable unit.
4. **The cut test is a real, runnable check.** Cut the turn; is the story the same? That
   is arithmetic a game can perform with the player.
5. **Praise is a move too.** FLA-02 exists to be praised specifically. A critique game that
   only finds faults teaches half of what he teaches.
