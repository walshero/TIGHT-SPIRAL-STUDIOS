Kireji Pond: the audition

Tap Play on each. Tell me a letter and a number, such as "voice C, や 2, win W2". Nothing changes in the game until you pick.

1 · The leap voice

Five takes on the yaaaa. The first three are voices; D is a small choir; E is an instrument, for when a voice from a machine is the weird part.

AWheee

The original idea done softer: a small, round voice rising like a child saying wheee, landing gently. No tremble.

BSlide whistle

The cartoon leap. A pure whistle slides up with a "ya" at the start. Instantly reads as a jump.

CKabuki call (YAH) · chosen

A kakegoe, the shout from the kabuki audience, with two wooden clappers (hyōshigi). Deeper, theatrical, and Japanese in its bones.

DFrog chorus

Three small voices in a bright major chord, all shouting yaaa together. A cheer rather than one voice.

EShakuhachi swoop

No voice at all: a breathy bamboo flute runs up a Japanese five-note scale and holds the top note.

FRecord your own

The least weird yaaaa is a real person. Record yours, or a student's with permission, and I will put that recording in the game.

2 · The や

Five ways to put the cutting word on screen. Each plays with the last voice you picked (B until you pick one).

3 · Win moments

For finishing a poem or a season. The research says to scale the celebration to the size of the win, so these run from small to big.

W1Coin chime

Two quick bright notes, a fourth apart: the Mario coin idea. Tiny and clean.

Best for: every correct catch or smash. Small, success-only feedback builds competence.

W2Koto cascade

Plucked strings tumble down a Japanese pentatonic scale and land on a chord. Fits the pond.

Best for: a finished poem.

W3Slot machine payout

Rolling bells, coins counting up, and a ding-ding-ding.

Use with care: casino sounds make players think they won more than they did. Only on a true win, never on a near miss.

W4Taiko finish

A drum roll that speeds up, one big don, and the kabuki "yoo".

Best for: finishing a season.

W5Slow-motion bloom

Two slow heartbeats, then a warm major chord opens and sparkles: the Peggle final-peg idea, without borrowing its music.

Best for: the last poem, before the frog leaps.

W6Temple bell · chosen

One deep bronze bell and a few drops into water. A quiet win, closest to Bashō.

Best for: the three-poem ending, where "water's sound" is the point.

My recommendation

Tie each size of win to one sound, success only: W1 on each right call, W2 on a finished poem, W4 on a finished season, and W6 with the frog's leap at the very end. Keep W3 out of a classroom game unless it plays only on a true win. For the voice, F, your own recording, beats any machine; of the synthesized ones, B reads as a leap fastest. For the や, 3 (pop with splash) has the best evidence behind it.

What the research says

  • Animated words work best paired with particles; meaningful words beat gibberish, and neither slows players down. Juicy Text (ICMI 2024)
  • Big feedback on success builds a sense of competence, but too much of it hides what the player actually did. Kao et al., CHI 2024
  • Winning sounds on slot machines made players remember winning more often than they did, including on losses dressed up as wins. Dixon et al., University of Waterloo · Journal of Gambling Studies
  • Decoration shapes how players feel about a game, not only how it looks. Hicks et al., Juicy Game Design, CHI PLAY 2019
  • The coin sound is two square-wave notes a fourth apart, B5 to E6. Super Mario Wiki
  • Peggle's final peg slows time and swells into Beethoven's "Ode to Joy". Peggle
