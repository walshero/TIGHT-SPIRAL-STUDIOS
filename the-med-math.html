The med math: the number a nurse has to get right.

A teaching tool, not clinical guidance. Fictional cases, rounded numbers.

On the floor, a dosage is not a homework problem: it is a real amount going into a real person, and the arithmetic is the last thing standing between an order and a mistake. You do the calculating; the page shows the work back and runs the safety check with you: set up the units, do the math, then check whether the number even makes sense. At the end you'll have an SBAR handoff you can say out loud.

Real dosing is verified against the order, the label, a second nurse, and your facility's protocol. Nobody gives a medication on the strength of a game. This is here to build the reasoning, not to replace the checks.

The whole skill, in one line

Set up the units so they cancel, do the arithmetic, then ask whether the answer makes sense. Most dosage errors are not arithmetic slips; they are answers nobody stopped to sanity-check. Eight tablets for one dose, a drip that would empty the bag in minutes, a pediatric dose bigger than an adult's: the math can be "right" and the number still wrong for the person in the bed.

That is why the last step is not the calculator. It is you, asking: is this a reasonable amount for this patient, this drug, this route? When the answer surprises you, stop and check. That pause is the skill.

Where this comes from. The methods are standard nursing dosage calculation: dimensional analysis and the formula method (Desired / Have × Quantity), IV rate as volume over time, and weight-based dosing (mg/kg × kg). The safety check follows the Rights of Medication Administration (right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation); SBAR is the standard handoff framework. Grounded in MassBay's Health Sciences programs at the Framingham campus. Source: standard nursing dosage-calculation and medication-safety practice; MassBay Fact Book (Health Sciences) and Health Sciences program pages. Last-verified 2026-08-08. Cases are fictional and rounded for teaching.
