While you edit — 7 authoring checks

  1. Describe the work, not the student. Anchor in observable features of the artifact, never in the person or their effort.
  2. Observable behaviors, not bare adjectives. Replace "excellent / good / poor" with what that quality looks like in the work.
  3. Parallel structure across levels. Each level varies the same attribute along a continuum — only the degree changes from 4 to 1.
  4. One construct per dimension. Don't fuse two ideas ("clear and organized"); a student can be one without the other. Split them.
  5. Criterion-based, not ranked or curved. Levels describe meeting a standard, not beating classmates. Every student can, in principle, earn a 4.
  6. Add a 0 / NA code. Give missing or off-task work an explicit code so a blank never gets silently averaged as a low score.
  7. Pilot before you norm. Run the draft against a few anonymized samples; fix the ambiguity in the tool, not in the meeting.

Draft vs. normed — what this does and doesn't do

Rubric Forge produces a draft, not a normed rubric. Drafting (here) is authoring: you define the dimensions, write the anchors, and localize the language. Its quality is content validity — do the criteria capture the outcome in words faculty can act on?

Norming (after, with people) is the inter-rater-reliability workshop: faculty independently score the same anonymized student work, compare, and revise the anchors until scorers land within an agreed tolerance. That's what earns the word "MassBay-normed." Until then, this is a draft to pilot — practice it on The Norming Table once it's ready.
