Frost's breaks

This poem is end-stopped. Almost every line finishes its own grammar, so the break and the sense land together and each line reads like a pronouncement. That is why cutting it felt findable even at speed. The sentence was helping you.

Both of those are real readings

A keeps the three-beat pulse and lets first go by lightly. B leans on first and gets four stresses in six syllables. Say them both out loud. A sounds like a rule being stated, B sounds like someone insisting. The line supports either, and which one you hear is a reading, not an error.

That is worth knowing before anyone hands you the words iamb and trochee. A foot is a name for a pattern somebody heard. Line 1 starts on a stressed syllable, NA-ture's, which is a trochee, and it is why the poem opens like a door being pushed rather than eased.

Your breaks

Frost's breaks

The sentence ran straight through all four line endings without pausing once. So the breaks are not doing grammar. They are doing something the punctuation cannot. Frost ends a line on wall, and the word hangs there a beat before the sentence picks it up again. He ends another on under it, and you sit in the dark under the wall for a moment before spills arrives.

End-stopped lines land like statements. Enjambed lines hand you a word, hold it, then keep going. Same poet, same meter, opposite feeling. The only difference is where the line stopped and the sentence did not.

Take it to your own draft

Flatten a poem you are working on into one prose block. Read it aloud and cut it where your breath cuts it. Then cut the same block again, on purpose, one word earlier than your breath wants, and see which word you just left hanging at the end of the line, and whether it deserved to be there.
