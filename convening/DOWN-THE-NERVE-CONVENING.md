## SEAT: Coordinator

Horvath's test: the neuroscience has to be the steering, not a quiz between runs. The
strongest evidence for that design rule is Habgood and Ainsworth (2011, Journal of the
Learning Sciences): children learned more from the version of Zombie Division where
the math was the combat mechanic, and in free play they chose it seven times longer
than the version where the math sat outside the game. So the belt speed is conduction
velocity, shown in meters per second on the toy's speedometer. The leap is saltatory
conduction. The lanes are fiber types. A player who goes faster on the insulated lane
has learned why myelin matters, whether or not they can name it yet.

Second rule: predict before you ride. Each route opens with one prediction the player
commits to with a single tap ("Which rider reaches the spinal cord first?"), and the
route itself answers it. Conceptual-change designs pose the prediction; refutation
designs name the wrong idea and then correct it. Refutation texts carry a moderate,
reliable effect across 44 comparisons (Schroeder and Kucera, 2022, g = 0.41). Each
route closes with one refutation card, two sentences at most, aimed at a real
misconception the run just disproved: "A nerve signal is electricity in a wire" (it is
regenerated at every node, and tops out near 120 m/s); "You feel pain the instant you
touch the stove" (the reflex pulls the hand first; feeling arrives later).

My veto, and it is a hard one: no brain-training claims, anywhere. This game teaches
how nerves work. It does not make your nerves faster, and the copy must never imply it
does. I have argued in print that the field must not replace one neuromyth with
another. The Your Own Nerve panel measures the player's reaction time to show where the
milliseconds go, not to rank or improve them. No scores carried between sessions, no
"personal best," no streaks.

Load ceiling: one new idea per route, never more than one sentence on screen during
motion, and the vocabulary word arrives after the player has felt the thing it names.
SIGNED Coordinator (Horvath), 2026-11-05

# RECONVENE 2026-11-05, 21:44 ET — STRESS, RESILIENCE, AND THE CHANGING NERVE

## SEAT: Domain Rep — is the founder's picture true? Yes, and it is better than true.

The landing places are real. Signals land on dendritic spines, the small receiving
points on the next neuron. Arnsten's review (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2009) reports
that with chronic stress the dendrites and spines of prefrontal cells retract while
dendrites in the amygdala expand. Radley et al. (Cerebral Cortex, 2006) measured a 16%
drop in spine density in rat medial prefrontal cortex after repeated stress. And it
comes back: Radley et al. (Experimental Neurology, 2005) found the retraction reversed
after three weeks of recovery. So "five pads becomes two" is a fair stage picture of
fewer spines, and "the pads grow back" is the resilience lesson, with evidence behind it.

Three corrections the build must carry. (1) The pads live at the gap between neurons
(the confluence), not along the insulated axon; nodes of Ranvier do not vanish under
stress. (2) There are two time scales. In the moment, stress chemistry weakens
prefrontal connections within minutes (Arnsten 2009); the game shows that as pads that
DIM and come back when the moment passes. Over a long hard stretch, pads VANISH and
take many rides to regrow. (3) Stress does not weaken everything. The same chemistry
strengthens the alarm circuits of the amygdala, so under stress the alarm road grows
MORE pads and pulls the spark toward it. Most structural evidence is from rodents; the
Library says so, and pad counts are labeled STUDIO SIMPLIFICATION.
SIGNED Domain Rep, 2026-11-05

## SEAT: Trauma-Informed Rep (new bench seat; source SAMHSA 2014, six principles)

SAMHSA names six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support;
collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; cultural, historical and
gender issues. Applied to this build:
- Safety: everyday stressors only (a test tomorrow, a phone buzzing at 2 a.m., a missed
  bus in the rain, a loud crowded room, a week of short nights). No violence, abuse,
  injury, loss or emergency imagery, ever. No jump scares.
- Transparency: every picture in the game is listed in the Library before play, and
  every change to the nerve has a "why" one tap away.
- Choice: the player can skip any picture, turn the outside layer off, or pick which
  stressors appear. The calm road is always one tap away.
- Empowerment: the alarm road is never the villain. It is the body protecting you. The
  lesson is balance and recovery, not "stress is bad, you failed." The spark always
  arrives; there is no game over.
- Peer support: one of the restorers is people (a friend on the steps, a phone call home).
- No blame: restorers are shown as things the world gives you, not homework you
  skipped. The game never asks the player how stressed they are and never infers it.
SIGNED Trauma-Informed Rep, 2026-11-05
