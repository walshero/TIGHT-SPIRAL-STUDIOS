## SEAT: Coordinator

Horvath's test: the memory work must be the play, not a quiz stapled to it. Three
design moves carry that. First, spacing. The gaps between the twelve evenings widen
(one week, then two, then a month, then a season), and the window outside shows the
season changing. Spaced practice across widening gaps is one of the most replicated
results in memory research (Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer, 2006, a review of
317 experiments). Second, retrieval before reveal. The player sets the room before the
doors open, from memory, and only then does the guest walk in. Recall comes first and
feedback comes second, never the other way around. Third, generation. At close the
player writes the host's notes for next time, choosing from what they saw, and those
notes are all they get next visit. Writing the notes is itself a retrieval, and reading
your own notes later is honest (the Wallers kept a book too). Load ceiling: no more
than three new facts across the whole room per evening, and never a fact the player
could not have seen or heard on stage. Nothing explains the lesson in a banner. The
empty chair on the next visit does the teaching.
SIGNED Coordinator (Horvath), 2026-09-29

## SEAT: Osterweil

Freedom to fail: a wrong setting costs one guest one visit. Nobody is scolded, the game
never ends early, and a guest who drifts away can drift back if the player gets them
right later. Freedom to experiment: the player can hold the salmon for someone who has
never ordered it, just to see. Freedom of identity: the player decides what kind of host
to be, one who knows everyone a little or one who knows two guests well. The plaque
forces that choice, because the room only ever gets one (the book is plain that no
other guest got a seat named after them). Freedom of effort: an evening can be short.
No points, no stars, no grade on screen. The only record is the wall calendar of twelve
evenings with the chairs filled or empty. Watch item: "comes less" must feel like weather,
not punishment. The guest never says why.
SIGNED Osterweil, 2026-09-29

## SEAT: Waller desk (domain rep, bench)

What the book knows that a designer would get wrong. A host does not say "the usual."
Regulars are named in the book by habit, not by story: Alan on Thursdays at 5:30, Trudy's
red wine and sparkling water, the save-a-stool guest, the couple who never book but own
Table 3. The bread plates on the high-top were the Wallers' real reservation signal for
Edgar, and they came off at a set time. Relationships are a trap the book names three
times: she is not his wife, she is not his mother, the younger man ordered for his
husband. A host who assumes loses the guest faster than a host who forgets. The book
also says a restaurant may never know why a guest stops coming, so the game must not
tell the player why either. Waller veto 2 goes to the Wallers with this build as the
question.
SIGNED Waller desk, 2026-09-29

## THE LOOP (what the seats agree the player does)

1. **Before the doors open.** The café sits empty. The player has three holds for the
   evening: put bread plates on a table, hold a portion off the count board, save a bar
   stool, set out crayons, pull a pot of coffee. They place them from memory, reading
   only their own three lines of notes from last time.
2. **Arrivals.** Guests come through the door one at a time. When a hold matches a
   guest, the recurring moment plays without a word: Nancy sits across from her father,
   the coffee is already there. When it misses, nothing bad happens tonight.
3. **The one choice at the table.** On arrival the host can greet by name, bring the
   drink without asking, or say the usual. Getting a relationship wrong (wife, mother,
   daughter) costs more than getting a detail blank.
4. **One new thing.** Each evening one or two guests reveal a fact on stage, in words
   and picture: a new job, a grandchild, a switch from red wine to sparkling.
5. **Close.** The host writes three lines of notes for next time from what the evening
   showed. At the set hour, unused bread plates come off the tables.
6. **The gap.** The window changes season. The calendar marks the evening. A guest who
   was missed two times running comes less; three and the chair stays empty.
7. **The plaque.** Once in the year the player may mount one plaque on one table. The
   room remembers it. The game ends on the plaque and stops.

## DECISIONS FOR THE FOUNDER (defaults proceed on "go")

- **D1. Permission.** Ask the Wallers whether the adaptation grant covers a second game
  and a public URL, and put veto 2 to them. Default: build as a Lab prototype, private
  until they answer.
- **D2. The real Edgar.** Edgar and Nancy are real people from the Wallers' café. Default:
  the playable cast are composites built from the book's habits (no real person's
  drinking, health or ending), and the true Edgar story appears once, as the Wallers'
  own words, on the closing card. The title keeps his name as the book's homage, with
  the Wallers' consent folded into D1.
- **D3. Setting.** Default: the suburban café (about sixty seats), because Edgar's
  high-top lived there. Alternative: the Berkshires inn with Thursday live music.
- **D4. Notes.** Default: three lines written at close, read before the next opening
  (Coordinator). Alternative: an unlimited notebook (Stranger's dissent).
- **D5. Length.** Default: twelve evenings across one year with widening gaps, about
  twelve to fifteen minutes in total, saved between sessions.
- **D6. Table Four link.** Default: the Table Four family is one of the regulars, and
  this café art is built so Table Four can mount it.
