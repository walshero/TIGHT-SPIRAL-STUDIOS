## THE QUESTION
v8.2 added two things to the two tightest beats: a **hunch** ("physical, or this room gets it?") *before* the reveal, and a **teaching line** *at* the reveal. Both were built to the signal review's spec. The spec is sound on paper. The groove check asks the different question: **playing it, does it still feel like play — or did we teach the fun out of it?**

## WHAT GROOVES (both lenses, unprompted)
- **The model itself swings.** The gradient is honest and *legible by feel* — slapstick booms with kids, dies on the cat; the reference bit climbs as the room "gets it." A player will *feel* that pattern before they can name it. Horvath: that's the generative liminal — a real thing discovered in the in-between, not a number handed down.
- **The benign line is dormant and honest.** Nothing edgy in the cast yet, so no fake "too much" verdicts. Cleese likes that we didn't manufacture a scold.
- **Scores stayed whispers.** The Maker line is one quiet sentence; nothing got a leaderboard. The earlier warning held.

## WHERE IT DOESN'T GROOVE (the honest part)

### Cleese — "you made them think *before* they feel"
Open mode is unhurried play; the tightest comic beat in the game is **bet → reveal** (anticipation → payoff). v8.2 slips an **analytical beat between them**: *diagnose why it'll carry.* That's a small step *out* of open mode — the player stops playing and starts explaining, right at the moment the anticipation should be building. It's one tap and it's optional, so it's a *lean*, not a break — but Cleese feels it: **we asked them to be a critic in the half-second they should be a kid.**

> And note the trap: the signal review *needs* the hunch **before** the reveal (an honest prediction can't be captured after the answer shows). So "capture the model cleanly" (the Aleph) and "let them feel first" (Cleese) genuinely **pull against each other.** That tension is the real finding — it's not a bug to patch, it's a **dial the founder sets.**

### Horvath — the cross can read as a soft grade
"*You bet on the room getting it — but it shares no language for that…*" is *true*, but the "**you bet … but**" shape is a **verdict**: you predicted, you were off. Even unscored, a binary right/wrong shadow falls on the hunch — and a right/wrong shadow is the first millimetre of the trickster slide (play → "what's the correct answer"). Horvath's line: **reveal what happened; don't adjudicate what they guessed.** The truth ("this room had no language for it, so the physical carried") is a *discovery*; the "you bet wrong" wrapper is a *mark*. Keep the first, drop the second, and the same fact opens a door instead of closing one.

## THE GROOVE, IN ONE LINE
> **It swings — the model is the good kind of liminal — but the instrumentation leans a half-step toward the head: it asks for analysis a beat too early (Cleese) and wraps the lesson in a faint verdict (Horvath). Loosen both and it's back in the pocket, without losing what the loop was for.**

## THREE WAYS TO SET THE DIAL (founder's call — it's a feel decision)
1. **Leave it (analytical, richest data).** Keep the hunch before the reveal and the cross as-is. Maximum learning signal; slightly heady. Fine if the game is a *trainer* first.
2. **Loosen the words, keep the shape (recommended).** Keep the hunch where it is (the instrumentation needs it) but (a) reframe it as a **dare, not a diagnosis** — e.g. "Call it: does the *body* carry this, or does the *room*?" — and (b) rewrite the teaching line to **pure revelation** — drop "you bet … but," keep "this room had no language for it — the body carried." Same data captured, the grade removed, the beat lightened. Best groove-for-cost.
3. **Feel first, reflect after (most open-mode, costs the clean signal).** Move the "why" to *after* the reveal as an optional "want to know why it landed?" — play first, lesson second. Grooviest for Cleese, but it forfeits the honest *prediction* (the reveal contaminates the answer), so the humor-literacy signal gets softer. A real trade.
